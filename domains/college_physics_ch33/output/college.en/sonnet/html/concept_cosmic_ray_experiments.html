🌸 Cosmic Ray Experiments

🌸 Cosmic Ray Experiments

Definition. Cosmic rays are highly energetic particles—primarily protons (~87%), alpha particles (~12%), and heavier nuclei—originating from astrophysical sources and impinging on Earth's atmosphere at relativistic velocities. Their energies span an enormous range, from roughly $10^9$ eV to beyond $10^{20}$ eV. Upon entering the upper atmosphere, a primary cosmic ray collides with an atmospheric nucleus (typically nitrogen or oxygen) and initiates a cascade of secondary particles known as an extensive air shower (EAS). The shower products include pions, muons, electrons, positrons, photons, and neutrinos, all propagating downward in a broad disk.

The flux of cosmic rays follows a power-law spectrum:

$$ \frac{dN}{dE} \propto E^{-\gamma} $$

where $\gamma \approx 2.7$ below the "knee" ($E \approx 3 \times 10^{15}$ eV) and $\gamma \approx 3.1$ above it, with a further spectral feature called the "ankle" near $10^{18.5}$ eV.

Worked Example. A primary proton of kinetic energy $K = 10^{15}$ eV strikes a stationary atmospheric proton. Determine the available center-of-mass energy $\sqrt{s}$.

Using the invariant $s = (E_1 + m_p c^2)^2 - (p_1 c)^2$, and noting that for an ultra-relativistic proton $E_1 \approx p_1 c \operatorname{gg} m_p c^2$:

$$ s \approx 2 m_p c^2 E_1 = 2(938\ \text{MeV})(10^{15}\ \text{eV}) \approx 1.88 \times 10^{18}\ \text{eV}^2 $$ $$ \sqrt{s} \approx 1.37 \times 10^9\ \text{eV} = 1.37\ \text{GeV} $$

This is far below the lab-frame energy, illustrating why fixed-target collisions are energetically inefficient.

Key Theorem (Greisen–Zatsepin–Kuzmin Limit). Protons with energies exceeding $E_{\text{GZK}} \approx 5 \times 10^{19}$ eV lose energy rapidly via pion photo-production on cosmic microwave background photons: $p + \gamma_{\text{CMB}} \rightarrow \Delta^+ \rightarrow p + \pi^0$. Consequently, ultra-high-energy cosmic rays from sources more than ~50 Mpc distant cannot reach Earth at these energies—a fundamental observational constraint.

Lab Exercise. Using a two-detector scintillator coincidence array separated vertically by 1.5 m, measure the muon count rate at sea level. Estimate the muon flux (particles m$^{-2}$ s$^{-1}$ sr$^{-1}$) and compare with the accepted value of approximately $70\ \text{m}^{-2}\text{s}^{-1}\text{sr}^{-1}$. Discuss how time-dilation allows sea-level detection of muons whose lab-frame lifetime is $\tau_\mu = 2.2\ \mu\text{s}$.
