🌱 Ionizing Radiation

🌱 Ionizing Radiation

Definition. Ionizing radiation refers to any form of electromagnetic radiation or particle emission that carries sufficient energy to liberate electrons from atoms or molecules, thereby producing ions. The threshold energy for ionization is approximately 10–33 eV, which corresponds roughly to the binding energy of valence electrons in most matter. Radiation above this threshold includes alpha particles ($\alpha$), beta particles ($\beta^-$ and $\beta^+$), gamma rays ($\gamma$), X-rays, and neutrons. Unlike non-ionizing radiation (visible light, radio waves), ionizing radiation can break chemical bonds and damage biological macromolecules such as DNA.

The energy of a photon is given by

$$ E = hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda} $$

where $h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J·s}$ is Planck's constant and $c$ is the speed of light. For radiation to ionize hydrogen (ionization energy 13.6 eV), the photon wavelength must satisfy $\lambda \leq 91\ \text{nm}$, placing the threshold in the far-ultraviolet regime.

Worked Example. A gamma ray emitted during $^{60}\text{Co}$ decay has an energy of 1.17 MeV. Determine how many hydrogen atoms this photon could ionize if all energy were transferred sequentially.

$$ N = \frac{E_\gamma}{E_{\text{ion}}} = \frac{1.17 \times 10^6\ \text{eV}}{13.6\ \text{eV}} \approx 8.6 \times 10^4 $$

Thus a single 1.17 MeV gamma photon carries enough energy to ionize roughly $86{,}000$ hydrogen atoms, illustrating why even a small radioactive source poses significant biological risk.

Key Theorem (Bragg–Gray Principle). The absorbed dose $D$ (in gray, Gy = J/kg) deposited in a medium is related to the ionization produced in a small gas cavity by

$$ D_{\text{medium}} = D_{\text{gas}} \cdot \frac{\bar{S}_{\text{medium}}}{\bar{S}_{\text{gas}}} $$

where $\bar{S}$ denotes the mean mass stopping power. This principle underpins all ionization-chamber dosimetry used in medical and industrial radiation monitoring.

🍃 Radiation Absorbed Dose

🍃 Radiation Absorbed Dose

Definition. When ionizing radiation passes through matter, it deposits energy by ionizing atoms and breaking molecular bonds. The radiation absorbed dose (RAD) quantifies this energy deposition per unit mass of the absorbing material. The SI unit is the gray (Gy), defined as

$$ 1\ \text{Gy} = 1\ \frac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}. $$

The older unit, the rad, remains common in clinical settings:

$$ 1\ \text{rad} = 0.01\ \text{Gy} = 10\ \text{mGy}. $$

Absorbed dose $D$ is formally written

$$ D = \frac{\Delta E}{\Delta m}, $$

where $\Delta E$ is the energy deposited in a mass element $\Delta m$. Because biological damage depends not only on the energy deposited but also on the type of radiation, absorbed dose is distinct from effective dose, which introduces a radiation weighting factor $w_R$.

Worked Example. A tissue sample of mass $m = 2.0\ \text{g}$ absorbs $\Delta E = 3.0 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{J}$ of gamma-ray energy during a diagnostic scan. Find the absorbed dose in grays and in rads.

$$ D = \frac{\Delta E}{m} = \frac{3.0 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{J}}{2.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{kg}} = 0.15\ \text{Gy}. $$

Converting to rads:

$$ D = 0.15\ \text{Gy} \times \frac{100\ \text{rad}}{1\ \text{Gy}} = 15\ \text{rad}. $$

This dose falls within the range typical of a single CT scan slice, illustrating the practical scale of diagnostic exposures.

Key Theorem (Linear Energy Transfer relationship). The absorbed dose rate $\dot{D}$ for a beam of particles is related to the particle fluence rate $\dot{\Phi}$ (particles per unit area per unit time) and the linear energy transfer $L$ (energy deposited per unit path length) by

$$ \dot{D} = \frac{\dot{\Phi}\, L}{\rho}, $$

where $\rho$ is the material density. This expression shows that dose increases with beam intensity and with the stopping power of the medium, a result central to radiation therapy treatment planning.

🍃 Relative Biological Effectiveness

🍃 Relative Biological Effectiveness

Definition. Not all ionizing radiation produces equal biological damage per unit of absorbed energy. The relative biological effectiveness (RBE) of a given radiation type is defined as the ratio of the absorbed dose of a reference radiation (typically 250 keV X-rays) required to produce a specified biological effect, to the absorbed dose of the radiation under study required to produce the same effect:

$$ \text{RBE} = \frac{D_{\text{ref}}}{D_{\text{test}}}\bigg|_{\text{same effect}} $$

RBE is dimensionless and depends on the radiation type, its energy, the tissue irradiated, and the biological endpoint under consideration. To account for varying biological potency, the biologically effective dose, called the dose equivalent $H$, is defined as

$$ H = \text{RBE} \times D $$

where $D$ is the absorbed dose in grays (Gy, $1\,\text{Gy} = 1\,\text{J/kg}$). The SI unit of dose equivalent is the sievert (Sv); thus $1\,\text{Sv} = 1\,\text{J/kg}$ of RBE-weighted energy deposition.

Worked Example. A radiation worker receives an absorbed dose of $2.0\,\text{mGy}$ from fast neutrons, for which $\text{RBE} = 10$, and simultaneously $5.0\,\text{mGy}$ from gamma rays ($\text{RBE} = 1$). Compute the total dose equivalent.

$$ H_{\text{neutrons}} = 10 \times 2.0\,\text{mGy} = 20\,\text{mSv} $$ $$ H_{\gamma} = 1 \times 5.0\,\text{mGy} = 5.0\,\text{mSv} $$ $$ H_{\text{total}} = 20\,\text{mSv} + 5.0\,\text{mSv} = 25\,\text{mSv} $$

Despite the neutron component carrying less than half the absorbed dose, it contributes 80% of the biological risk.

Key Theorem. The linear energy transfer (LET) of a particle — the energy deposited per unit path length — governs RBE. High-LET radiations (alpha particles, heavy ions, fast neutrons) produce dense ionization tracks that overwhelm cellular DNA repair mechanisms, yielding RBE values ranging from 10 to 20. Low-LET radiations (X-rays, gamma rays, electrons) produce sparse ionization and are assigned $\text{RBE} \approx 1$.

🍃 Therapeutic Ratio

🍃 Therapeutic Ratio

Definition. The therapeutic ratio (TR) is the quantitative measure of the clinical utility of a radiation treatment modality, defined as the ratio of the tumor control probability (TCP) to the normal tissue complication probability (NTCP):

$$ \text{TR} = \frac{\text{TCP}}{\text{NTCP}} $$

A therapeutic ratio greater than unity indicates that the treatment is more likely to eradicate the tumor than to produce serious damage to surrounding healthy tissue. In practice, clinicians seek treatment protocols that maximize TR by exploiting differential radiosensitivity between malignant cells and normal tissue, or by delivering dose with geometric precision.

Worked Example. Suppose a radiation oncologist is comparing two treatment plans for a deep-seated tumor. Plan A delivers a dose of 60 Gy to the tumor with a TCP of 0.85 and produces an NTCP of 0.30 in adjacent critical organs. Plan B, using intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT), achieves the same TCP of 0.85 while reducing the NTCP to 0.10 through tighter beam collimation. Compute and compare the therapeutic ratios.

For Plan A:

$$ \text{TR}_A = \frac{0.85}{0.30} \approx 2.83 $$

For Plan B:

$$ \text{TR}_B = \frac{0.85}{0.10} = 8.5 $$

Plan B yields a therapeutic ratio three times larger, confirming that geometrical dose conformity substantially improves clinical outcomes without sacrificing tumor control.

Key Theorem. The therapeutic ratio is enhanced whenever the relative biological effectiveness (RBE) of the chosen radiation differs between tumor and normal tissue. If $\text{RBE}_{\text{tumor}} > \text{RBE}_{\text{normal}}$, then for equal absorbed doses (in Gy), the biologically effective dose $D_{\text{eff}} = D \cdot \text{RBE}$ is higher in the tumor, shifting TCP upward while holding NTCP constant, thus increasing TR. Heavy-ion beams (e.g., carbon-12) exploit the Bragg peak to achieve precisely this condition.

🍃 Dose Equivalent

🍃 Dose Equivalent

Definition. When ionizing radiation interacts with biological tissue, the physical quantity of absorbed dose alone does not fully characterize the biological hazard. Different radiation types cause different degrees of cellular damage even at identical absorbed doses. The dose equivalent $H$ is defined to account for this variation:

$$ H = D \cdot Q $$

where $D$ is the absorbed dose (in gray, $1\,\text{Gy} = 1\,\text{J/kg}$) and $Q$ is the dimensionless quality factor (also called the radiation weighting factor $w_R$ in the modern ICRP framework). The SI unit of dose equivalent is the sievert (Sv); the older unit is the rem, with the conversion $1\,\text{Sv} = 100\,\text{rem}$.

Representative quality factors are: $Q = 1$ for X-rays, gamma rays, and beta particles; $Q = 5$–$20$ for neutrons (energy-dependent); and $Q = 20$ for alpha particles. Alpha particles are therefore substantially more dangerous per unit absorbed dose than gamma radiation, a critical consideration in internal contamination scenarios.

Worked Example. A radiation worker receives an absorbed dose of $0.050\,\text{Gy}$ from fast neutrons ($Q = 10$) and $0.020\,\text{Gy}$ from gamma rays ($Q = 1$). The total dose equivalent is

$$ H = (0.050\,\text{Gy})(10) + (0.020\,\text{Gy})(1) = 0.50\,\text{Sv} + 0.020\,\text{Sv} = 0.52\,\text{Sv}. $$

Because dose equivalents from different radiation types are additive, this single value characterizes the combined biological risk to the worker.

Key Theorem (Linearity of Dose Equivalent). For a mixed radiation field consisting of $n$ components each with absorbed dose $D_i$ and quality factor $Q_i$, the total dose equivalent is

$$ H_{\text{total}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} D_i\, Q_i. $$

This linearity, valid within the linear no-threshold (LNT) model, underpins occupational exposure limits. The annual effective dose limit for radiation workers is $50\,\text{mSv}$ per year (ICRP recommendation), with a five-year average not exceeding $20\,\text{mSv/year}$.

🍃 Radiotherapy

🍃 Radiotherapy

Definition. Radiotherapy is the clinical application of ionizing radiation to destroy or inhibit the proliferation of malignant tissue. The underlying mechanism exploits the high linear energy transfer (LET) of radiation, which induces irreparable double-strand breaks in the DNA of rapidly dividing cancer cells. Common modalities include external beam radiation therapy (EBRT) using photon beams (X-rays or gamma rays), proton therapy, and brachytherapy (internal source implantation). The absorbed dose $D$ delivered to tissue is measured in grays: $1\ \text{Gy} = 1\ \text{J/kg}$. Because different radiation types cause varying degrees of biological damage per unit energy deposited, the biologically weighted quantity is the equivalent dose $H = D \cdot Q$, expressed in sieverts (Sv), where $Q$ is the radiation weighting factor ($Q = 1$ for photons, $Q = 20$ for alpha particles).

Worked Example. A tumor receives a dose of $2.0\ \text{Gy}$ per fraction from a $^{60}\text{Co}$ gamma-ray source ($Q = 1$). The equivalent dose per fraction is:

$$ H = D \cdot Q = (2.0\ \text{Gy})(1) = 2.0\ \text{Sv}. $$

Over a standard course of 30 fractions, the total equivalent dose to the tumor is:

$$ H_\text{total} = 30 \times 2.0\ \text{Sv} = 60\ \text{Sv}. $$

This cumulative dose is lethal to most tumor cells, while healthy surrounding tissue is protected by fractionation — the deliberate delivery of small daily doses — which allows normal cells to undergo sublethal damage repair between sessions.

Key Theorem — Therapeutic Ratio. The effectiveness of radiotherapy is quantified by the therapeutic ratio (TR), defined as:

$$ \text{TR} = \frac{\text{dose required to control tumor}}{\text{dose causing unacceptable normal tissue damage}}. $$

A TR $> 1$ indicates a favorable treatment window. Proton therapy exploits the Bragg peak, a sharp maximum in energy deposition at a precisely tunable depth, sharply reducing dose distal to the tumor and thereby improving TR compared to photon EBRT.

🌸 Cancer Radiotherapy

🌸 Cancer Radiotherapy

Definition. Cancer radiotherapy is the clinical application of ionizing radiation to destroy malignant cells by depositing sufficient energy to damage DNA beyond the cell's capacity for repair. The key physical quantity is the absorbed dose, $D$, defined as the energy deposited per unit mass of tissue:

$$ D = \frac{\Delta E}{\Delta m}, \quad [D] = \text{Gy} \equiv \text{J kg}^{-1}. $$

Because biological damage depends on radiation type, the dose equivalent $H$ weights the absorbed dose by the radiation weighting factor $w_R$:

$$ H = w_R \, D, \quad [H] = \text{Sv (sievert)}. $$

For photons and electrons $w_R = 1$; for protons $w_R \approx 2$; for alpha particles $w_R = 20$. The therapeutic ratio quantifies treatment efficacy as the ratio of tumor-cell kill to normal-tissue damage — maximizing this ratio is the central engineering challenge of radiotherapy.

Worked Example. A tumor of mass $m = 25\text{ g}$ is irradiated with a $^{60}$Co gamma source delivering $\Delta E = 2.5 \times 10^{-2}\text{ J}$ to the tumor volume. Find the absorbed dose and dose equivalent.

$$ D = \frac{\Delta E}{m} = \frac{2.5 \times 10^{-2}\text{ J}}{25 \times 10^{-3}\text{ kg}} = 1.0\text{ Gy}. $$

Since $^{60}$Co emits gamma rays ($w_R = 1$):

$$ H = w_R \, D = (1)(1.0\text{ Gy}) = 1.0\text{ Sv}. $$

A curative course typically delivers $60$–$70\text{ Gy}$ in fractions of $\approx 2\text{ Gy}$ per session, exploiting the differential repair capacity between healthy and tumor tissue.

Key Theorem (Bragg Peak Principle). Heavy charged particles (protons, carbon ions) lose energy according to the Bethe–Bloch relation, depositing a sharply localized maximum — the Bragg peak — at depth $R \propto E_0^{1.77}/\rho$, where $E_0$ is the initial kinetic energy and $\rho$ is tissue density. Tuning $E_0$ positions the peak precisely at tumor depth, sparing overlying healthy tissue and delivering negligible dose beyond the target.

Payoff 🎯

Cancer radiotherapy represents the most direct translation of nuclear and radiation physics into clinical medicine, uniting every major concept developed in Chapter 32 — radioactive decay, ionizing radiation, absorbed dose, relative biological effectiveness, and neutron moderation — into a single, life-saving intervention. It is the natural endpoint of this chapter because it demands not merely qualitative understanding but precise quantitative reasoning: a physicist must calculate delivered dose to within a few percent, account for tissue heterogeneity, and exploit the differential radiosensitivity between malignant and healthy cells to achieve tumor control while sparing surrounding organs.

At its core, radiotherapy works by depositing sufficient energy in tumor tissue to cause lethal double-strand breaks in DNA. The dose equivalent $H$ received by a target volume is given by

$$ H = D \cdot w_R $$

where $D$ is the absorbed dose in gray ($1\,\text{Gy} = 1\,\text{J\,kg}^{-1}$) and $w_R$ is the radiation weighting factor encoding relative biological effectiveness. For a 6 MV photon beam used in external-beam radiotherapy, $w_R = 1$, so $H = D$; for fast neutron therapy, $w_R \approx 10$, meaning a tenfold smaller physical dose produces equivalent biological damage.

Worked example. A tumor of mass $m = 50\,\text{g}$ receives a prescribed dose of $D = 2\,\text{Gy}$ per fraction from a $^{60}$Co gamma source. The energy deposited per fraction is

$$ E = D \cdot m = 2\,\text{J\,kg}^{-1} \times 0.050\,\text{kg} = 0.10\,\text{J}. $$

Over a standard 30-fraction course this totals $3.0\,\text{J}$ — a quantity so small it would raise tissue temperature by less than $0.01\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, yet it is sufficient to sterilize the tumor because the damage is radiochemical, not thermal.

The concept of the therapeutic ratio — maximizing tumor dose while minimizing normal-tissue dose — connects radiotherapy to every application domain in the chapter: radioactive isotope production (for brachytherapy seeds such as $^{125}$I), neutron moderation physics (for boron neutron capture therapy), and the quantitative dosimetry built on decay-law calculations.
