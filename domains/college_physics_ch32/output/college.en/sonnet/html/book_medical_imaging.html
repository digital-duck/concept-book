🌱 Radioactive Decay

🌱 Radioactive Decay

Definition. Radioactive decay is the spontaneous transformation of an unstable atomic nucleus into a more stable configuration, accompanied by the emission of particles or electromagnetic radiation. The process is governed by quantum-mechanical tunneling and is entirely random at the level of individual nuclei. For a large ensemble of $N$ identical nuclei, the decay rate is proportional to the number present:

$$ \frac{dN}{dt} = -\lambda N $$

where $\lambda$ (s$^{-1}$) is the decay constant, a characteristic of the nuclide. Solving this first-order linear ODE gives the exponential decay law:

$$ N(t) = N_0 \, e^{-\lambda t} $$

with $N_0$ the initial population. The half-life $t_{1/2}$ — the time for half the nuclei to decay — relates to $\lambda$ by:

$$ t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda} \approx \frac{0.693}{\lambda} $$

The activity $A = \lambda N$ (measured in becquerels, $1\,\text{Bq} = 1\,\text{decay s}^{-1}$) decreases as $A(t) = A_0 e^{-\lambda t}$.

Worked Example. Iodine-131 ($^{131}$I), used in thyroid cancer treatment, has $t_{1/2} = 8.02\,\text{days}$. A clinical dose delivers an initial activity of $A_0 = 3.70 \times 10^8\,\text{Bq}$. Find the activity after 24 days.

First, compute the decay constant:

$$ \lambda = \frac{0.693}{8.02\,\text{days}} = 0.0864\,\text{day}^{-1} $$

Then evaluate:

$$ A(24) = 3.70 \times 10^8 \times e^{-0.0864 \times 24} = 3.70 \times 10^8 \times e^{-2.074} $$ $$ A(24) = 3.70 \times 10^8 \times 0.1257 \approx 4.65 \times 10^7\,\text{Bq} $$

After three half-lives, roughly $\tfrac{1}{8}$ of the original activity remains, consistent with this result.

Key Theorem (Bateman Condition). For a decay chain $A \to B \to C$, secular equilibrium is reached when $t_{1/2}(A) \operatorname{gg} t_{1/2}(B)$. Under this condition, $A_A = A_B$: the daughter activity equals the parent activity, and the daughter population remains constant in time.

🍃 Tagging

🍃 Tagging

Definition. Radioactive tagging (also called radioactive labeling or tracing) is the technique of incorporating a radioisotope into a molecule or compound so that the substance can be tracked in situ by detecting its emitted radiation. The tagged atom is chemically identical to its stable counterpart, so it participates in biological, chemical, or physical processes without perturbation, while its radioactive decay provides a continuous, detectable signal. The fundamental requirement is that the radioisotope's half-life $T_{1/2}$ be long enough to complete the experiment yet short enough to minimize radiation dose to the subject. The activity $A$ of a tagged sample follows

$$ A(t) = A_0 \, e^{-\lambda t}, \qquad \lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{T_{1/2}}, $$

where $A_0$ is the initial activity and $\lambda$ is the decay constant.

Worked Example. A physician administers $^{99\text{m}}\text{Tc}$ (technetium-99m, $T_{1/2} = 6.01\,\text{h}$) tagged to a phosphonate compound for a bone scan. The injected activity is $A_0 = 740\,\text{MBq}$. Find the activity remaining after 24 h.

$$ \lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{6.01\,\text{h}} = 0.1153\,\text{h}^{-1} $$ $$ A(24\,\text{h}) = 740\,\text{MBq} \times e^{-(0.1153)(24)} = 740 \times e^{-2.767} \approx 740 \times 0.0630 \approx 46.6\,\text{MBq} $$

After four half-lives the activity has fallen to roughly $\frac{1}{16}$ of its initial value, demonstrating that $^{99\text{m}}\text{Tc}$ clears rapidly while still providing adequate signal during the imaging window.

Key Theorem. The minimum detectable quantity of a tagged substance is governed by the specific activity $S_A = \lambda N_A / M$, where $N_A$ is Avogadro's number and $M$ is the molar mass. Because $\lambda$ can be large even for nanogram quantities, radioactive tagging can detect concentrations far below those accessible by conventional chemical analysis — a sensitivity advantage of many orders of magnitude over stable tracers.

🌱 Ionizing Radiation

🌱 Ionizing Radiation

Definition. Ionizing radiation refers to any form of electromagnetic radiation or particle emission that carries sufficient energy to liberate electrons from atoms or molecules, thereby producing ions. The threshold energy for ionization is approximately 10–33 eV, which corresponds roughly to the binding energy of valence electrons in most matter. Radiation above this threshold includes alpha particles ($\alpha$), beta particles ($\beta^-$ and $\beta^+$), gamma rays ($\gamma$), X-rays, and neutrons. Unlike non-ionizing radiation (visible light, radio waves), ionizing radiation can break chemical bonds and damage biological macromolecules such as DNA.

The energy of a photon is given by

$$ E = hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda} $$

where $h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J·s}$ is Planck's constant and $c$ is the speed of light. For radiation to ionize hydrogen (ionization energy 13.6 eV), the photon wavelength must satisfy $\lambda \leq 91\ \text{nm}$, placing the threshold in the far-ultraviolet regime.

Worked Example. A gamma ray emitted during $^{60}\text{Co}$ decay has an energy of 1.17 MeV. Determine how many hydrogen atoms this photon could ionize if all energy were transferred sequentially.

$$ N = \frac{E_\gamma}{E_{\text{ion}}} = \frac{1.17 \times 10^6\ \text{eV}}{13.6\ \text{eV}} \approx 8.6 \times 10^4 $$

Thus a single 1.17 MeV gamma photon carries enough energy to ionize roughly $86{,}000$ hydrogen atoms, illustrating why even a small radioactive source poses significant biological risk.

Key Theorem (Bragg–Gray Principle). The absorbed dose $D$ (in gray, Gy = J/kg) deposited in a medium is related to the ionization produced in a small gas cavity by

$$ D_{\text{medium}} = D_{\text{gas}} \cdot \frac{\bar{S}_{\text{medium}}}{\bar{S}_{\text{gas}}} $$

where $\bar{S}$ denotes the mean mass stopping power. This principle underpins all ionization-chamber dosimetry used in medical and industrial radiation monitoring.

🍃 Radiopharmaceutical

🍃 Radiopharmaceutical

Definition. A radiopharmaceutical is a compound in which one or more atoms are radioactive nuclides, designed so that the molecule selectively localizes in a specific tissue or organ following administration to a patient. The radiopharmaceutical serves a dual purpose: the pharmaceutical component governs biodistribution (where the compound accumulates), while the radionuclide component emits ionizing radiation that either enables diagnostic imaging or delivers a therapeutic radiation dose to the target site. Formally, if $N(t)$ is the number of radionuclide atoms present at time $t$, the activity is

$$ A(t) = \lambda N(t) = A_0 e^{-\lambda t}, $$

where $\lambda = \ln 2 / T_{1/2}$ is the decay constant and $T_{1/2}$ is the physical half-life. In vivo, the effective elimination of a radiopharmaceutical combines physical decay and biological clearance, yielding an effective half-life

$$ T_{\text{eff}} = \frac{T_{1/2}\, T_b}{T_{1/2} + T_b}, $$

where $T_b$ is the biological half-life.

Worked Example. Technetium-99m ($^{99\text{m}}$Tc) is the most widely used diagnostic radiopharmaceutical, with $T_{1/2} = 6.01\ \text{h}$. Suppose a patient is administered $A_0 = 740\ \text{MBq}$ of $^{99\text{m}}$Tc-labeled sestamibi for a cardiac perfusion scan, and the biological half-life in myocardial tissue is $T_b = 12.0\ \text{h}$. The effective half-life is

$$ T_{\text{eff}} = \frac{6.01 \times 12.0}{6.01 + 12.0} \approx 4.01\ \text{h}. $$

After $t = 8.0\ \text{h}$, the remaining activity in tissue is

$$ A(8.0) = 740\, e^{-(\ln 2 / 4.01)(8.0)} \approx 740 \times 0.251 \approx 186\ \text{MBq}. $$

Key Theorem (ALARA Principle and Dosimetry). The absorbed dose $D$ delivered by a radiopharmaceutical to a target organ of mass $m$ is

$$ D = \frac{\tilde{A}\, \Delta}{m}, $$

where $\tilde{A} = \int_0^\infty A(t)\, dt = A_0 / \lambda_{\text{eff}}$ is the cumulated activity and $\Delta$ is the mean energy emitted per nuclear transition. Minimizing $T_{\text{eff}}$ reduces $\tilde{A}$ and therefore reduces $D$, consistent with the ALARA (As Low As Reasonably Achievable) principle.

Discuss why $^{131}$I is preferable to a longer-lived iodine isotope for therapeutic applications.

🍃 Anger Camera

🍃 Anger Camera

Definition

The Anger camera (also called a gamma camera or scintillation camera) is an imaging device used in nuclear medicine to detect gamma radiation emitted from a radiopharmaceutical distributed within a patient's body. Invented by Hal Anger in 1958, it converts spatially distributed gamma-ray emission into a two-dimensional intensity map, enabling clinicians to assess organ function and detect pathological processes non-invasively.

The camera consists of three principal components: a collimator, a large-area scintillation crystal (typically sodium iodide doped with thallium, NaI(Tl)), and an array of photomultiplier tubes (PMTs). The collimator—a lead plate perforated by parallel channels—admits only gamma photons traveling perpendicular to its face, establishing geometric correspondence between emission location in the body and position on the detector face. A gamma photon that passes through the collimator strikes the NaI(Tl) crystal, producing a burst of scintillation photons with intensity proportional to the deposited energy. Multiple PMTs simultaneously detect this light burst; the position of the original interaction is determined by a centroid calculation (Anger logic):

$$ x = \frac{\sum_i S_i x_i}{\sum_i S_i}, \qquad y = \frac{\sum_i S_i y_i}{\sum_i S_i} $$

where $S_i$ is the signal from the $i$-th PMT located at coordinates $(x_i, y_i)$.

Worked Example

Suppose a patient receives $^{99\text{m}}$Tc ($T_{1/2} = 6.01\ \text{h}$, $E_\gamma = 140\ \text{keV}$) for a thyroid scan with an initial activity of $A_0 = 185\ \text{MBq}$. After $t = 6.01\ \text{h}$, the activity has decayed to $A = A_0 / 2 = 92.5\ \text{MBq}$. At 140 keV, the gamma ray falls within the energy window accepted by the Anger camera's pulse-height analyzer, ensuring that only photoelectric events—not scattered photons—contribute to the image, preserving spatial fidelity.

Key Theorem

The spatial resolution $R$ of an Anger camera is governed by the collimator geometry:

$$ R \approx d\left(1 + \frac{b}{L}\right) $$

where $d$ is the collimator hole diameter, $L$ is the hole length, and $b$ is the source-to-collimator distance. Resolution degrades linearly with distance $b$, establishing a fundamental trade-off between patient standoff and image sharpness. Sensitivity (count rate per unit activity) scales as $d^2/L^2$, creating an inherent sensitivity–resolution compromise that drives collimator selection in clinical protocols.

🍃 Pet

🍃 Pet

Definition. Positron Emission Tomography (PET) is a nuclear medical imaging technique that exploits the annihilation of positrons ($\beta^+$ particles) with electrons to produce pairs of coincident 511 keV gamma-ray photons. A radiopharmaceutical labeled with a positron-emitting isotope — most commonly fluorine-18 ($^{18}_{9}\text{F}$, $t_{1/2} = 109.8\ \text{min}$) — is administered to the patient. Each nuclear decay produces a positron that travels only a short distance ($\sim$1–3 mm) before annihilating with a nearby electron via the reaction:

$$ e^+ + e^- \rightarrow 2\gamma, \quad E_\gamma = m_e c^2 = 0.511\ \text{MeV} $$

By conservation of momentum, the two photons are emitted in exactly opposite directions ($180°$ apart). A ring of scintillation detectors surrounding the patient records coincident photon pairs and reconstructs a three-dimensional map of metabolic activity through filtered back-projection or iterative algorithms.

Worked Example. A patient receives $^{18}_{9}\text{F}$-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) with an initial activity of $A_0 = 370\ \text{MBq}$. Determine the activity remaining after one half-life and calculate the energy deposited per annihilation event.

Activity after one half-life:

$$ A = A_0 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{t/t_{1/2}} = 370\ \text{MBq} \times \frac{1}{2} = 185\ \text{MBq} $$

Energy per annihilation: Each of the two photons carries $E = 0.511\ \text{MeV}$, so the total energy released per event is:

$$ E_\text{total} = 2 \times 0.511\ \text{MeV} = 1.022\ \text{MeV} = 1.637 \times 10^{-13}\ \text{J} $$

Key Theorem — Collinearity Condition. The spatial resolution and coincidence detection of PET rest on four-momentum conservation. In the center-of-mass frame where the positron and electron are essentially at rest, the total momentum is zero. Therefore the two photons must satisfy:

$$ \vec{p}_{\gamma_1} + \vec{p}_{\gamma_2} = 0 \implies |\vec{p}_{\gamma_1}| = |\vec{p}_{\gamma_2}|, \quad \theta = 180° $$

Any angular deviation from collinearity (caused by residual kinetic energy) contributes an intrinsic limit of roughly 1–2 mm to spatial resolution.

🍃 Spect

🍃 SPECT

Definition. Single-Photon Emission Computed Tomography (SPECT) is a nuclear medicine imaging modality in which a gamma-emitting radiopharmaceutical is administered to a patient and the resulting photon flux is detected by one or more rotating scintillation cameras. Unlike planar gamma-camera imaging, SPECT acquires projection data from multiple angles and applies filtered back-projection (or iterative reconstruction) to produce three-dimensional tomographic maps of radiotracer distribution. The technique exploits the fact that technetium-99m ($^{99\text{m}}$Tc), the most widely used SPECT agent, decays by isomeric transition, emitting a single 140 keV gamma photon with a half-life of $T_{1/2} = 6.0\,\text{h}$, which is energetically ideal for detection while minimizing patient dose.

Worked Example. A patient receives $370\,\text{MBq}$ of $^{99\text{m}}$Tc-sestamibi for a cardiac perfusion study. Determine the activity remaining after one full camera rotation lasting $20\,\text{min}$.

Using the exponential decay law,

$$ A(t) = A_0\,e^{-\lambda t}, \qquad \lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{T_{1/2}} = \frac{0.6931}{360\,\text{min}} = 1.925\times10^{-3}\,\text{min}^{-1}. $$

After $t = 20\,\text{min}$:

$$ A(20) = 370\,\text{MBq}\times e^{-(1.925\times10^{-3})(20)} = 370\times e^{-0.03850} \approx 370\times 0.9622 \approx 356\,\text{MBq}. $$

Less than 4% of activity is lost during acquisition, so the reconstruction approximation of constant source strength introduces negligible error.

Key Theorem (Projection-Slice Theorem). The one-dimensional Fourier transform of a projection $p(\xi,\phi)$ taken at angle $\phi$ equals a central slice through the two-dimensional Fourier transform $F(u,v)$ of the activity distribution $f(x,y)$ at the same angle. Formally,

$$ \mathcal{F}_1\{p(\xi,\phi)\}(\omega) = F(\omega\cos\phi,\,\omega\sin\phi). $$

This theorem underpins filtered back-projection: multiplying each projection spectrum by the ramp filter $|\omega|$ before back-projecting compensates for the $1/r$ density of sampling in frequency space and yields an unbiased reconstruction.

🌸 Medical Imaging

🌸 Medical Imaging

Definition. Medical imaging encompasses a collection of physics-based techniques that produce spatial maps of internal anatomical structures or physiological processes without invasive surgery. The primary modalities — X-ray radiography, computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), positron emission tomography (PET), and ultrasound — each exploit a distinct physical interaction between a probe (electromagnetic radiation, radiofrequency fields, or mechanical waves) and biological tissue. Image contrast arises from spatially varying material properties such as electron density, nuclear spin relaxation time, or acoustic impedance.

In X-ray radiography, transmitted intensity follows Beer–Lambert attenuation:

$$ I = I_0 \, e^{-\mu x} $$

where $I_0$ is the incident photon fluence, $\mu$ is the linear attenuation coefficient of the tissue ($\text{m}^{-1}$), and $x$ is the path length through the material. Bone ($\mu \approx 100\ \text{m}^{-1}$ at 60 keV) attenuates far more strongly than soft tissue ($\mu \approx 20\ \text{m}^{-1}$), producing the characteristic bright skeletal regions on a film or digital detector.

Worked Example. A diagnostic chest X-ray uses 80 keV photons. A beam passes through 3.0 cm of lung tissue ($\mu = 18\ \text{m}^{-1}$) and then 0.50 cm of rib bone ($\mu = 95\ \text{m}^{-1}$). Find the fraction of photons transmitted.

$$ \frac{I}{I_0} = e^{-\mu_{\text{lung}} x_{\text{lung}}} \cdot e^{-\mu_{\text{bone}} x_{\text{bone}}} $$ $$ = e^{-(18)(0.030)} \cdot e^{-(95)(0.0050)} = e^{-0.54} \cdot e^{-0.475} \approx 0.583 \times 0.622 \approx 0.36 $$

Approximately 36% of photons reach the detector behind the rib, while 63% reach the detector behind pure lung tissue, generating measurable contrast between the two regions.

Key Theorem (Radon Inversion). CT reconstruction rests on the Radon transform: a full set of line-integral projections $p(\phi, s)$ acquired at angles $\phi$ uniquely determines the 2-D attenuation map $\mu(x,y)$ via filtered back-projection. The reconstruction filter in frequency space applies a ramp kernel $|k|$ before back-projecting each projection, restoring spatial resolution lost by simple summation.

Payoff 🎯

Medical imaging stands as the culminating achievement of nuclear and radiation physics because it transforms abstract principles — radioactive decay, ionizing radiation, quantum tunneling, and nuclear interactions — into direct, life-saving observation of the living human body. Throughout Chapter 32, each concept has been a building block: the exponential decay law governs how radioisotopes vanish from tissue; radiation absorbed dose and dose equivalent quantify the biological cost of every diagnostic procedure; neutron moderation underlies the reactor physics that produces medical radioisotopes in the first place. Medical imaging is not merely an application of these ideas — it is their synthesis, the point at which the physics of the nucleus becomes the physics of diagnosis.

Consider positron emission tomography (PET). A patient receives a positron-emitting tracer such as $^{18}\text{F}$-fluorodeoxyglucose. Each $\beta^+$ decay produces a positron that annihilates with a nearby electron, emitting two $\gamma$ photons in precisely opposite directions ($\theta = 180°$, each carrying energy $E = m_e c^2 = 0.511\ \text{MeV}$). Coincidence detectors reconstruct emission locations via the relation $\Delta t = \Delta x / c$, producing a three-dimensional metabolic map with spatial resolution approaching $\sim 4\ \text{mm}$. The dose equivalent received by the patient — typically $\sim 7\ \text{mSv}$ for a whole-body scan — is weighed against diagnostic benefit using the quality factor framework introduced earlier in the chapter.

Key theorem (ALARA principle): For any imaging protocol, the administered activity $A_0$ should satisfy

$$ H_\text{eff} = \sum_i w_T \cdot w_R \cdot \frac{A_0 \cdot \tau_\text{eff} \cdot S_i}{m_i} \leq H_\text{limit}, $$

where $w_T$ and $w_R$ are tissue and radiation weighting factors, $\tau_\text{eff}$ is the effective half-life, $S_i$ the organ-specific absorbed fraction, and $m_i$ the organ mass. Minimizing $A_0$ while preserving image contrast is the central optimization problem of nuclear medicine.
