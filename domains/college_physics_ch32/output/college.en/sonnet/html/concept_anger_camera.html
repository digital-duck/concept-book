🍃 Anger Camera

🍃 Anger Camera

Definition

The Anger camera (also called a gamma camera or scintillation camera) is an imaging device used in nuclear medicine to detect gamma radiation emitted from a radiopharmaceutical distributed within a patient's body. Invented by Hal Anger in 1958, it converts spatially distributed gamma-ray emission into a two-dimensional intensity map, enabling clinicians to assess organ function and detect pathological processes non-invasively.

The camera consists of three principal components: a collimator, a large-area scintillation crystal (typically sodium iodide doped with thallium, NaI(Tl)), and an array of photomultiplier tubes (PMTs). The collimator—a lead plate perforated by parallel channels—admits only gamma photons traveling perpendicular to its face, establishing geometric correspondence between emission location in the body and position on the detector face. A gamma photon that passes through the collimator strikes the NaI(Tl) crystal, producing a burst of scintillation photons with intensity proportional to the deposited energy. Multiple PMTs simultaneously detect this light burst; the position of the original interaction is determined by a centroid calculation (Anger logic):

$$ x = \frac{\sum_i S_i x_i}{\sum_i S_i}, \qquad y = \frac{\sum_i S_i y_i}{\sum_i S_i} $$

where $S_i$ is the signal from the $i$-th PMT located at coordinates $(x_i, y_i)$.

Worked Example

Suppose a patient receives $^{99\text{m}}$Tc ($T_{1/2} = 6.01\ \text{h}$, $E_\gamma = 140\ \text{keV}$) for a thyroid scan with an initial activity of $A_0 = 185\ \text{MBq}$. After $t = 6.01\ \text{h}$, the activity has decayed to $A = A_0 / 2 = 92.5\ \text{MBq}$. At 140 keV, the gamma ray falls within the energy window accepted by the Anger camera's pulse-height analyzer, ensuring that only photoelectric events—not scattered photons—contribute to the image, preserving spatial fidelity.

Key Theorem

The spatial resolution $R$ of an Anger camera is governed by the collimator geometry:

$$ R \approx d\left(1 + \frac{b}{L}\right) $$

where $d$ is the collimator hole diameter, $L$ is the hole length, and $b$ is the source-to-collimator distance. Resolution degrades linearly with distance $b$, establishing a fundamental trade-off between patient standoff and image sharpness. Sensitivity (count rate per unit activity) scales as $d^2/L^2$, creating an inherent sensitivity–resolution compromise that drives collimator selection in clinical protocols.

Laboratory Exercise

Position a point source of $^{99\text{m}}$Tc at distances $b = 5,\ 10,\ 15\ \text{cm}$ from a parallel-hole collimator. For each distance, acquire a 60-second image and determine the full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) of the point-spread function by fitting a Gaussian to the intensity profile. Plot FWHM versus $b$ and verify the linear relationship predicted by the resolution formula above. Report the slope $d/L$ and compare it to the manufacturer's collimator specifications.
