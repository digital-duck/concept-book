🍃 Radiation Absorbed Dose

🍃 Radiation Absorbed Dose

Definition. When ionizing radiation passes through matter, it deposits energy by ionizing atoms and breaking molecular bonds. The radiation absorbed dose (RAD) quantifies this energy deposition per unit mass of the absorbing material. The SI unit is the gray (Gy), defined as

$$ 1\ \text{Gy} = 1\ \frac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}. $$

The older unit, the rad, remains common in clinical settings:

$$ 1\ \text{rad} = 0.01\ \text{Gy} = 10\ \text{mGy}. $$

Absorbed dose $D$ is formally written

$$ D = \frac{\Delta E}{\Delta m}, $$

where $\Delta E$ is the energy deposited in a mass element $\Delta m$. Because biological damage depends not only on the energy deposited but also on the type of radiation, absorbed dose is distinct from effective dose, which introduces a radiation weighting factor $w_R$.

Worked Example. A tissue sample of mass $m = 2.0\ \text{g}$ absorbs $\Delta E = 3.0 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{J}$ of gamma-ray energy during a diagnostic scan. Find the absorbed dose in grays and in rads.

$$ D = \frac{\Delta E}{m} = \frac{3.0 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{J}}{2.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{kg}} = 0.15\ \text{Gy}. $$

Converting to rads:

$$ D = 0.15\ \text{Gy} \times \frac{100\ \text{rad}}{1\ \text{Gy}} = 15\ \text{rad}. $$

This dose falls within the range typical of a single CT scan slice, illustrating the practical scale of diagnostic exposures.

Key Theorem (Linear Energy Transfer relationship). The absorbed dose rate $\dot{D}$ for a beam of particles is related to the particle fluence rate $\dot{\Phi}$ (particles per unit area per unit time) and the linear energy transfer $L$ (energy deposited per unit path length) by

$$ \dot{D} = \frac{\dot{\Phi}\, L}{\rho}, $$

where $\rho$ is the material density. This expression shows that dose increases with beam intensity and with the stopping power of the medium, a result central to radiation therapy treatment planning.

Lab Exercise. Using a calibrated Geiger-Müller counter and a known $^{137}\text{Cs}$ source, position the detector at distances $r = 10,\ 20,\ 30\ \text{cm}$ from the source. Record the count rate at each distance, apply the inverse-square law to estimate the photon fluence rate, and use the published mass-energy absorption coefficient for air ($\mu_{en}/\rho \approx 0.0268\ \text{m}^2\,\text{kg}^{-1}$ at 662 keV) to calculate the absorbed dose rate in air at each position. Compare your results with the manufacturer's activity specification and discuss sources of discrepancy, including attenuation and geometry corrections.
