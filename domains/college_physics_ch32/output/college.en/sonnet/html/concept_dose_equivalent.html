🍃 Dose Equivalent

🍃 Dose Equivalent

Definition. When ionizing radiation interacts with biological tissue, the physical quantity of absorbed dose alone does not fully characterize the biological hazard. Different radiation types cause different degrees of cellular damage even at identical absorbed doses. The dose equivalent $H$ is defined to account for this variation:

$$ H = D \cdot Q $$

where $D$ is the absorbed dose (in gray, $1\,\text{Gy} = 1\,\text{J/kg}$) and $Q$ is the dimensionless quality factor (also called the radiation weighting factor $w_R$ in the modern ICRP framework). The SI unit of dose equivalent is the sievert (Sv); the older unit is the rem, with the conversion $1\,\text{Sv} = 100\,\text{rem}$.

Representative quality factors are: $Q = 1$ for X-rays, gamma rays, and beta particles; $Q = 5$–$20$ for neutrons (energy-dependent); and $Q = 20$ for alpha particles. Alpha particles are therefore substantially more dangerous per unit absorbed dose than gamma radiation, a critical consideration in internal contamination scenarios.

Worked Example. A radiation worker receives an absorbed dose of $0.050\,\text{Gy}$ from fast neutrons ($Q = 10$) and $0.020\,\text{Gy}$ from gamma rays ($Q = 1$). The total dose equivalent is

$$ H = (0.050\,\text{Gy})(10) + (0.020\,\text{Gy})(1) = 0.50\,\text{Sv} + 0.020\,\text{Sv} = 0.52\,\text{Sv}. $$

Because dose equivalents from different radiation types are additive, this single value characterizes the combined biological risk to the worker.

Key Theorem (Linearity of Dose Equivalent). For a mixed radiation field consisting of $n$ components each with absorbed dose $D_i$ and quality factor $Q_i$, the total dose equivalent is

$$ H_{\text{total}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} D_i\, Q_i. $$

This linearity, valid within the linear no-threshold (LNT) model, underpins occupational exposure limits. The annual effective dose limit for radiation workers is $50\,\text{mSv}$ per year (ICRP recommendation), with a five-year average not exceeding $20\,\text{mSv/year}$.

Lab Exercise. A Geiger counter near a sealed ${}^{241}\text{Am}$ source reads an absorbed dose rate of $2.0\,\mu\text{Gy/hr}$ in tissue-equivalent material. Given that americium-241 decays primarily by alpha emission ($Q = 20$), calculate the dose equivalent rate in $\mu\text{Sv/hr}$. Then determine how many hours of continuous unshielded exposure would reach the annual occupational limit of $50\,\text{mSv}$. Discuss why the alpha source poses a negligible external hazard but a severe internal ingestion hazard.
