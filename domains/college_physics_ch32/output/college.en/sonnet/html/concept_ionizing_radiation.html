🌱 Ionizing Radiation

🌱 Ionizing Radiation

Definition. Ionizing radiation refers to any form of electromagnetic radiation or particle emission that carries sufficient energy to liberate electrons from atoms or molecules, thereby producing ions. The threshold energy for ionization is approximately 10–33 eV, which corresponds roughly to the binding energy of valence electrons in most matter. Radiation above this threshold includes alpha particles ($\alpha$), beta particles ($\beta^-$ and $\beta^+$), gamma rays ($\gamma$), X-rays, and neutrons. Unlike non-ionizing radiation (visible light, radio waves), ionizing radiation can break chemical bonds and damage biological macromolecules such as DNA.

The energy of a photon is given by

$$ E = hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda} $$

where $h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J·s}$ is Planck's constant and $c$ is the speed of light. For radiation to ionize hydrogen (ionization energy 13.6 eV), the photon wavelength must satisfy $\lambda \leq 91\ \text{nm}$, placing the threshold in the far-ultraviolet regime.

Worked Example. A gamma ray emitted during $^{60}\text{Co}$ decay has an energy of 1.17 MeV. Determine how many hydrogen atoms this photon could ionize if all energy were transferred sequentially.

$$ N = \frac{E_\gamma}{E_{\text{ion}}} = \frac{1.17 \times 10^6\ \text{eV}}{13.6\ \text{eV}} \approx 8.6 \times 10^4 $$

Thus a single 1.17 MeV gamma photon carries enough energy to ionize roughly $86{,}000$ hydrogen atoms, illustrating why even a small radioactive source poses significant biological risk.

Key Theorem (Bragg–Gray Principle). The absorbed dose $D$ (in gray, Gy = J/kg) deposited in a medium is related to the ionization produced in a small gas cavity by

$$ D_{\text{medium}} = D_{\text{gas}} \cdot \frac{\bar{S}_{\text{medium}}}{\bar{S}_{\text{gas}}} $$

where $\bar{S}$ denotes the mean mass stopping power. This principle underpins all ionization-chamber dosimetry used in medical and industrial radiation monitoring.

Lab Exercise. Using a Geiger–Müller tube, measure the count rate from a $^{137}\text{Cs}$ source at distances of 5, 10, 20, and 40 cm. Plot count rate versus $r^{-2}$ and verify the inverse-square law. Then interpose 1 mm and 3 mm aluminum sheets between source and detector, and determine the half-value layer for the 662 keV gamma emission.
