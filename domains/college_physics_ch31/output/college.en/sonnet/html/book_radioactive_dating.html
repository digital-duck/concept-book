🌱 Nucleus

🌱 Nucleus

Definition. The atomic nucleus is the dense, positively charged core of an atom, composed of protons and neutrons (collectively called nucleons) bound together by the strong nuclear force. For an element with atomic number $Z$ and mass number $A$, the nucleus contains $Z$ protons and $N = A - Z$ neutrons. The nuclear radius is empirically described by

$$ r = r_0 A^{1/3}, $$

where $r_0 \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-15}\ \text{m} = 1.2\ \text{fm}$. This relationship implies that nuclear volume scales as $A$, so nuclear density is approximately constant across all nuclides — a striking consequence of the nearly incompressible nature of nuclear matter.

Because nuclear matter resists compression, the volume occupied by each nucleon is nearly the same regardless of which nucleus it inhabits. This uniformity underlies much of nuclear phenomenology and distinguishes the strong nuclear force from gravitational or electrostatic interactions, neither of which produces a saturating, constant-density bound state.

Worked Example. Estimate and compare the radii of ${}^{12}_6\text{C}$ and ${}^{238}_{92}\text{U}$.

For carbon: $r_C = 1.2 \times (12)^{1/3}\ \text{fm} = 1.2 \times 2.289\ \text{fm} \approx 2.75\ \text{fm}$.

For uranium: $r_U = 1.2 \times (238)^{1/3}\ \text{fm} = 1.2 \times 6.204\ \text{fm} \approx 7.44\ \text{fm}$.

The ratio of radii is $(238/12)^{1/3} \approx 2.71$, while the ratio of mass numbers is $238/12 \approx 19.8$. The cube-root scaling keeps nuclear radii far more similar in size than the mass numbers alone might suggest, confining all known nuclei to the range of roughly $1$–$8\ \text{fm}$.

Key Theorem (Constant Nuclear Density). The density of nuclear matter follows directly from the radius formula. Taking each nucleon to have mass $m_N \approx 1.67 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$ and the nuclear volume as $V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r_0^3 A$, the density is

$$ \rho = \frac{A m_N}{\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r_0^3 A} = \frac{3 m_N}{4\pi r_0^3} \approx 2.3 \times 10^{17}\ \text{kg/m}^3, $$

independent of $A$. This is roughly $10^{14}$ times the density of ordinary matter and is the same density found in neutron stars.

🌱 Proton

🌱 Proton

Definition. A proton is a positively charged subatomic particle found in the nucleus of every atom. It carries a charge of $+e$, where the elementary charge is $e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$, and possesses a rest mass of $m_p = 1.673 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$, approximately 1836 times the electron mass. Protons are classified as baryons — composite particles made of three quarks bound together by the strong nuclear force. The number of protons in a nucleus defines the atomic number $Z$, which uniquely identifies the element.

Worked Example. Consider a proton accelerated from rest through a potential difference of $\Delta V = 5.00\ \text{kV}$. Using conservation of energy, the work done on the proton equals its gain in kinetic energy:

$$ q\,\Delta V = \tfrac{1}{2}m_p v^2 $$

Solving for the final speed:

$$ v = \sqrt{\frac{2e\,\Delta V}{m_p}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(1.602\times10^{-19})(5.00\times10^3)}{1.673\times10^{-27}}} $$ $$ v \approx 9.79 \times 10^5\ \text{m/s} $$

This speed ($\approx 0.3\%$ of $c$) confirms that a classical treatment is adequate here, since relativistic corrections become significant only above roughly $10\%$ of $c$.

Key Theorem — Magnetic Deflection. A proton moving with velocity $v$ perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field $B$ undergoes uniform circular motion. Equating the magnetic Lorentz force to the centripetal requirement:

$$ evB = \frac{m_p v^2}{r} \implies r = \frac{m_p v}{eB} $$

The ratio $r = m_p v/(eB)$ is called the magnetic rigidity, and it relates the radius of curvature directly to the proton's momentum. This relation is fundamental to particle accelerator design and mass spectrometry.

🌱 Neutron

🌱 Neutron

Definition. The neutron is an electrically neutral subatomic particle residing in the nucleus of atoms. It carries a rest mass of $m_n = 1.674927 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$, nearly identical to the proton mass $m_p$, and a baryon number of $+1$. Despite its zero net charge, the neutron has a nonzero magnetic moment ($\mu_n = -1.9130\ \mu_N$), evidence of internal structure: in the Standard Model, the neutron is composed of one up quark and two down quarks ($udd$), yielding a net charge of $\frac{2}{3}e - \frac{1}{3}e - \frac{1}{3}e = 0$. A free neutron is unstable and undergoes beta-minus decay—the one new process introduced here—in which a down quark converts to an up quark via the weak force, producing a proton, an electron, and an electron antineutrino:

$$ n \rightarrow p + e^- + \bar{\nu}_e $$

The mean lifetime of a free neutron is $\tau_n \approx 879\ \text{s}$. Inside a stable nucleus, the residual strong force suppresses this decay.

Worked Example. Consider carbon-14, $^{14}_{6}\text{C}$. With atomic number $Z = 6$ and mass number $A = 14$, the neutron count is:

$$ N = A - Z = 14 - 6 = 8 $$

Using the measured atomic mass $m(^{14}\text{C}) = 14.003242\ \text{u}$ along with $m_p = 1.007276\ \text{u}$ and $m_n = 1.008665\ \text{u}$, the mass deficit is $\Delta m = 6(1.007276) + 8(1.008665) - 14.003242 = 0.11298\ \text{u}$, giving a total binding energy $BE = 0.11298 \times 931.5\ \text{MeV} \approx 105.2\ \text{MeV}$, or about $7.51\ \text{MeV}$ per nucleon.

Key Theorem (Neutron Moderation). Fast neutrons ($\sim 1\ \text{MeV}$) must be slowed to thermal energies ($\sim 0.025\ \text{eV}$) to sustain a fission chain reaction efficiently. For elastic collisions with a moderator nucleus of mass number $A$, the fractional energy transferred per collision is $\Delta E/E = 4A/(1+A)^2$, which is maximized at $A = 1$. This makes hydrogen the most effective moderator per collision.

🍃 Atomic Number

🍃 Atomic Number

Definition. The atomic number of an element, denoted $Z$, is the number of protons contained within the nucleus of an atom of that element. Because each proton carries a charge of $+e = +1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$, the total nuclear charge is $Q = Ze$. In a neutral atom, the number of orbiting electrons equals $Z$, so the atomic number simultaneously determines the element's chemical identity and its electronic structure. The atomic number is a dimensionless positive integer ranging from $Z = 1$ (hydrogen) to $Z = 118$ (oganesson) for currently known elements.

Worked Example. Consider the element oxygen. Its atomic number is $Z = 8$, meaning every oxygen nucleus contains exactly 8 protons, giving a nuclear charge of

$$ Q = Ze = 8 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C} = 1.282 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{C}. $$

A neutral oxygen atom therefore has 8 electrons distributed in shells: $1s^2\ 2s^2\ 2p^4$. The nucleus may also contain neutrons; the neutron number $N$ can vary, producing isotopes with different mass numbers $A = Z + N$ but identical chemical behavior. For oxygen-16, $N = 8$ and $A = 16$; for oxygen-18, $N = 10$ and $A = 18$, yet both share $Z = 8$.

Key Theorem (Moseley's Law). Henry Moseley demonstrated in 1913 that the square root of the characteristic X-ray frequencies emitted by an element is linearly proportional to its atomic number:

$$ \sqrt{f} = a(Z - b), $$

where $a$ and $b$ are experimentally determined constants. This relation confirmed that $Z$, not atomic mass, is the fundamental organizing quantity of the periodic table, and it allowed gaps corresponding to undiscovered elements to be identified unambiguously. For $K_\alpha$ X-rays, $b \approx 1$ and $a \approx 4.97 \times 10^7\ \text{Hz}^{1/2}$.

🍃 Nucleon

🍃 Nucleon

Definition. A nucleon is any particle that resides in the atomic nucleus; the term encompasses both the proton and the neutron. Protons carry electric charge $+e = +1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$, while neutrons are electrically neutral. Both particles have nearly equal rest masses:

$$ m_p = 1.007276\ \text{u}, \qquad m_n = 1.008665\ \text{u}, $$

where $1\ \text{u} = 1.66054 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$ is the unified atomic mass unit. The total number of nucleons in a nucleus is the mass number $A$, which equals the sum of the atomic number $Z$ (proton count) and the neutron number $N$:

$$ A = Z + N. $$

Nucleons are bound together by the strong nuclear force, a short-range ($\sim 1\ \text{fm} = 10^{-15}\ \text{m}$) attractive interaction that overwhelms electrostatic repulsion at sub-femtometer separations.

Worked Example. Consider the nuclide $^{56}_{26}\text{Fe}$, ordinary iron. Here $A = 56$ and $Z = 26$, so the number of neutrons is

$$ N = A - Z = 56 - 26 = 30. $$

The nucleus therefore contains 26 protons and 30 neutrons, for a total of 56 nucleons. The nuclear binding energy per nucleon—a measure of stability—is approximately $8.8\ \text{MeV}$, among the highest of any nuclide, which is why iron represents the endpoint of stellar nucleosynthesis.

Key Theorem (Semi-Empirical Mass Formula). The Bethe–Weizsäcker formula approximates the nuclear binding energy $B$ as

$$ B = a_v A - a_s A^{2/3} - a_c \frac{Z(Z-1)}{A^{1/3}} - a_a \frac{(A - 2Z)^2}{A} + \delta(A,Z), $$

where the terms represent, respectively, volume, surface, Coulomb, asymmetry, and pairing contributions. This formula encodes the fact that binding energy scales extensively with nucleon number $A$ in the nuclear interior, reflecting the short-range saturation property of the strong force.

🍃 Mass Number

🍃 Mass Number

Definition. The mass number of a nuclide, denoted $A$, is the total count of nucleons—protons and neutrons—contained within the atomic nucleus. If $Z$ denotes the atomic number (proton count) and $N$ the neutron number, then

$$ A = Z + N. $$

The mass number is always a positive integer and appears as the superscript in standard nuclide notation ${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$, where X is the chemical symbol. Because electrons contribute negligibly to nuclear mass ($m_e \approx 0.00055\,\text{u}$), $A$ serves as a useful integer approximation to the atomic mass expressed in unified atomic mass units (u), where $1\,\text{u} = 1.6605 \times 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}$.

Worked Example. Consider the nuclide carbon-14, written ${}^{14}_{6}\text{C}$. Here $Z = 6$ (carbon always has six protons) and $A = 14$, so

$$ N = A - Z = 14 - 6 = 8 \text{ neutrons.} $$

The actual atomic mass of ${}^{14}\text{C}$ is $14.003242\,\text{u}$; the small deviation from the integer $A = 14$ arises from nuclear binding energy (via $E = \Delta m\, c^2$) and the electron masses. This deviation is central to nuclear energy calculations, but its detailed treatment belongs to the binding-energy discussion that follows.

Key Theorem — Conservation of Mass Number. In any nuclear reaction or decay process, the total mass number is conserved:

$$ \sum A_{\text{reactants}} = \sum A_{\text{products}}. $$

For example, in the alpha decay of uranium-238,

$$ {}^{238}_{92}\text{U} \;\longrightarrow\; {}^{234}_{90}\text{Th} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He}, $$

the mass numbers satisfy $238 = 234 + 4$, and the atomic numbers satisfy $92 = 90 + 2$. This conservation law, combined with charge conservation, constrains which decay and reaction channels are energetically and physically accessible for a given nuclide.

🌱 Mass Energy Equivalence

🌱 Mass Energy Equivalence

Definition

Mass-energy equivalence is the principle that mass and energy are interconvertible manifestations of a single physical quantity, related by Einstein's equation:

$$ E_0 = mc^2 $$

where $E_0$ denotes the rest energy of an object, $m$ its rest mass, and $c = 2.998 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s}$ the speed of light in vacuum. This relation emerges directly from special relativity. Recall from earlier in this chapter that the total relativistic energy of a moving particle is $E = \gamma mc^2$, where $\gamma = (1 - v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$ is the Lorentz factor introduced previously. At rest ($v = 0$, $\gamma = 1$), this expression reduces to $E_0 = mc^2$: the rest energy. The rest energy represents an enormous reservoir locked within mass itself, entirely independent of kinetic or potential energy, and constitutes the single new primitive of this section.

Worked Example

Determine the rest energy of a proton, given its rest mass $m_p = 1.673 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$.

$$ E_0 = m_p c^2 = (1.673 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg})(2.998 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s})^2 $$ $$ E_0 = 1.503 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{J} = \frac{1.503 \times 10^{-10}}{1.602 \times 10^{-19}}\ \text{eV} \approx 938.3\ \text{MeV} $$

This is the standard proton rest energy quoted throughout nuclear and particle physics. The conversion factor $1\ \text{eV} = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$ is essential for expressing nuclear energies in convenient units.

Key Theorem: Conservation of Relativistic Energy

In any isolated system, the total relativistic energy $E_{\text{total}} = \sum_i \gamma_i m_i c^2$ is conserved. A direct corollary is that the measured mass of a bound nucleus is strictly less than the sum of its constituent nucleon masses—a difference accounted for entirely by $E_0 = mc^2$ applied to the energy released during assembly. For helium-4, this accounts for approximately $28.3\ \text{MeV}$, confirming that rest energy governs nuclear stability without requiring any concept beyond $E_0 = mc^2$.

🍃 Nuclide

🍃 Nuclide

A nuclide is a specific nuclear species characterized by a unique combination of proton number $Z$ (the atomic number), neutron number $N$, and nuclear energy state. It is formally denoted as $^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$, where $\text{X}$ is the chemical symbol of the element, $A = Z + N$ is the mass number (total nucleon count), and $Z$ specifies the element's identity. Two nuclides sharing the same $Z$ but differing in $N$ are called isotopes. A nuclide in an excited nuclear energy state is called a nuclear isomer, denoted $^{A\text{m}}_{Z}\text{X}$.

Worked Example. Consider carbon. The nuclide $^{12}_{6}\text{C}$ has $Z = 6$ protons and $N = 6$ neutrons, giving $A = 12$. The nuclide $^{14}_{6}\text{C}$, by contrast, has $Z = 6$ and $N = 8$, making it an isotope of carbon with $A = 14$. Because $^{14}_{6}\text{C}$ has an unfavorable neutron-to-proton ratio, it is radioactive and undergoes $\beta^{-}$ decay:

$$ ^{14}_{6}\text{C} \;\longrightarrow\; ^{14}_{7}\text{N} + e^{-} + \bar{\nu}_e $$

Here, a neutron converts to a proton, increasing $Z$ from 6 to 7 while $A$ remains 14, producing the stable nuclide $^{14}_{7}\text{N}$.

Key Theorem (Nuclear Stability). Stable nuclides tend to cluster along the band of stability in a plot of $N$ versus $Z$. For light nuclides ($Z \leq 20$), stability occurs near $N \approx Z$. For heavier nuclides, the Coulomb repulsion among protons requires an excess of neutrons for stability, shifting the band toward $N > Z$. Nuclides outside this band decay via $\alpha$, $\beta^{-}$, $\beta^{+}$, or electron-capture processes until a stable configuration is reached.

🌱 Conservation Of Energy

🌱 Conservation Of Energy

Definition. The law of conservation of energy states that the total energy of an isolated system remains constant over time. In nuclear physics, this principle encompasses rest-mass energy through Einstein's mass-energy equivalence, so the conserved quantity in any reaction is the sum of kinetic energies and rest-mass energies of all participants. The single new primitive introduced here is the Q-value, which quantifies the net energy released or absorbed in a nuclear reaction:

$$ Q = \left(\sum m_{\text{reactants}} - \sum m_{\text{products}}\right)c^2 $$

A positive $Q$ indicates an exothermic reaction in which rest mass is converted to kinetic energy; a negative $Q$ indicates an endothermic reaction that requires an external energy input at least equal to $|Q|$ to proceed.

Worked Example. Consider the alpha decay of radium-226:

$$ {}^{226}_{88}\text{Ra} \rightarrow {}^{222}_{86}\text{Rn} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} $$

Using atomic masses: $m({}^{226}\text{Ra}) = 226.025410\ \text{u}$, $m({}^{222}\text{Rn}) = 222.017578\ \text{u}$, $m({}^{4}\text{He}) = 4.002602\ \text{u}$.

$$ \Delta m = 226.025410 - (222.017578 + 4.002602) = 0.005230\ \text{u} $$

Converting using $1\ \text{u} = 931.5\ \text{MeV}/c^2$:

$$ Q = 0.005230 \times 931.5\ \text{MeV} = 4.87\ \text{MeV} $$

This energy appears primarily as kinetic energy of the alpha particle. By momentum conservation, the much heavier radon nucleus carries only a small recoil fraction, so the alpha particle receives approximately $4.78\ \text{MeV}$, consistent with experimentally measured alpha energies from this decay.

Key Theorem. For any nuclear reaction, if the total rest mass of reactants exceeds that of products ($\Delta m > 0$), kinetic energy $Q = \Delta m \cdot c^2$ is released. If $\Delta m < 0$, the reaction is endothermic and requires a threshold kinetic energy of at least $|Q|$ in the center-of-mass frame to proceed. This threshold condition governs the minimum beam energies required in particle accelerator experiments.

🌱 Conservation Of Momentum

🌱 Conservation Of Momentum

Definition. The law of conservation of momentum states that the total linear momentum of an isolated system remains constant in time. Formally, for a system of $n$ particles with no net external force acting on it:

$$ \frac{d\vec{p}_{\text{total}}}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i \vec{v}_i = \vec{0} $$

This follows directly from Newton's third law: every internal force between particles in the system occurs as an action-reaction pair of equal magnitude and opposite direction, so all internal contributions to $d\vec{p}/dt$ cancel. The conserved quantity is $\vec{p}_{\text{total}} = \sum_i m_i \vec{v}_i$, and in component form each of $p_x$, $p_y$, and $p_z$ is independently conserved when the corresponding net external force component is zero.

Worked Example. A stationary uranium-238 nucleus ($m_U = 238\,\text{u}$) undergoes alpha decay, emitting an alpha particle ($m_\alpha = 4\,\text{u}$) with speed $v_\alpha = 1.50 \times 10^7\,\text{m/s}$. Find the recoil speed of the daughter thorium-234 nucleus ($m_{Th} = 234\,\text{u}$).

Before decay, $\vec{p}_{\text{total}} = \vec{0}$. After decay, conservation of momentum requires:

$$ m_\alpha v_\alpha - m_{Th} v_{Th} = 0 $$ $$ v_{Th} = \frac{m_\alpha}{m_{Th}} v_\alpha = \frac{4}{234}(1.50 \times 10^7\,\text{m/s}) \approx 2.56 \times 10^5\,\text{m/s} $$

The daughter nucleus recoils in the direction opposite the alpha particle, consistent with $\vec{p}_{\text{total}} = \vec{0}$.

Key Theorem. For any two-body collision in one dimension—regardless of the internal forces exchanged—total momentum is conserved as long as no net external force acts during the interaction:

$$ m_1 v_{1i} + m_2 v_{2i} = m_1 v_{1f} + m_2 v_{2f} $$

When the target is initially at rest ($v_{2i} = 0$) and the two objects stick together (a perfectly inelastic collision), the single unknown final velocity is $v_f = \dfrac{m_1}{m_1 + m_2}v_{1i}$. This result holds for any mass ratio, demonstrating that momentum conservation alone—without any assumption about energy—fully determines the outcome of a perfectly inelastic event.

🍃 Radioactivity

🍃 Radioactivity

Definition. Radioactivity is the spontaneous emission of radiation from an unstable atomic nucleus as it transitions toward a more energetically favorable configuration. This process is governed entirely by quantum mechanics and is independent of chemical state, temperature, or pressure. A nucleus may shed energy by emitting a helium-4 nucleus (alpha emission), an electron or positron paired with a neutrino (beta emission), or a high-energy photon (gamma emission); in each case charge, baryon number, and lepton number are conserved. These emission modes are manifestations of the single underlying phenomenon — radioactive decay — rather than distinct primitives.

The rate of decay follows first-order kinetics. If $N(t)$ denotes the number of undecayed nuclei at time $t$, then

$$ \frac{dN}{dt} = -\lambda N, $$

where $\lambda$ is the decay constant characteristic of the nuclide. The solution is

$$ N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}, $$

with $N_0$ the initial count. The half-life $t_{1/2}$ is the time required for $N$ to fall to half its initial value:

$$ t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}. $$

Worked Example. Iodine-131 ($^{131}_{53}$I) has a half-life of $t_{1/2} = 8.02$ days. Starting with a $10.0\,\mu\text{g}$ sample, find the mass remaining after 24.06 days.

Number of half-lives elapsed: $n = 24.06/8.02 = 3.00$. Therefore,

$$ m = m_0\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^n = 10.0\times\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^3 = 1.25\,\mu\text{g}. $$

Key Theorem — Radioactive Decay Law. For any radioactive species, the activity $A = \lambda N$ also decays exponentially: $A(t) = A_0 e^{-\lambda t}$. This follows directly from differentiating $N(t)$. Because $A$ is directly measurable (in becquerels, $1\,\text{Bq} = 1\,\text{decay}\,\text{s}^{-1}$), the decay law enables experimental determination of $\lambda$ without knowing the absolute number of atoms.

🍃 Nuclear Decay

🍃 Nuclear Decay

Definition. Nuclear decay is the spontaneous transformation of an unstable atomic nucleus into a more stable configuration through the emission of particles or electromagnetic radiation. The process is governed by conservation laws (charge, baryon number, lepton number, energy-momentum) and is inherently probabilistic: the decay rate of a sample of $N$ identical nuclei is proportional to $N$ itself,

$$ \frac{dN}{dt} = -\lambda N, $$

where $\lambda$ is the decay constant (units: s$^{-1}$). Integrating yields the fundamental law of radioactive decay:

$$ N(t) = N_0\, e^{-\lambda t}. $$

The half-life $t_{1/2} = \ln 2\, /\, \lambda$ is the time required for half of any initial population to decay. The activity $A = \lambda N$ (measured in becquerels, 1 Bq = 1 decay s$^{-1}$) quantifies the instantaneous decay rate.

Worked Example. Carbon-14 ($^{14}_{6}\text{C}$) undergoes $\beta^-$ decay with $t_{1/2} = 5730\,\text{yr}$, so $\lambda = \ln 2 / 5730\,\text{yr} \approx 1.21 \times 10^{-4}\,\text{yr}^{-1}$. Suppose an archaeological sample initially contains $N_0 = 6.0\times10^{10}$ atoms of $^{14}$C. After $t = 11{,}460\,\text{yr}$ (exactly two half-lives), the remaining population is

$$ N = N_0\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^2 = 6.0\times10^{10} \times 0.25 = 1.5\times10^{10}\,\text{atoms}. $$

The activity at that time is $A = \lambda N = (1.21\times10^{-4}\,\text{yr}^{-1})(1.5\times10^{10}) \approx 1.82\times10^6\,\text{yr}^{-1} \approx 0.058\,\text{Bq}$.

Key Theorem (Bateman Equations). For a decay chain $A \to B \to C$ with constants $\lambda_A$ and $\lambda_B$, the population of daughter nuclide $B$ evolves as

$$ N_B(t) = \frac{\lambda_A N_{A,0}}{\lambda_B - \lambda_A}\!\left(e^{-\lambda_A t} - e^{-\lambda_B t}\right) + N_{B,0}\,e^{-\lambda_B t}. $$

When $\lambda_B \operatorname{gg} \lambda_A$, secular equilibrium is reached: $\lambda_A N_A = \lambda_B N_B$, meaning daughter activity matches parent activity.

🍃 Half Life

🍃 Half Life

Definition. The half-life $t_{1/2}$ of a radioactive nuclide is the time interval after which exactly one-half of any given initial quantity of that nuclide has undergone radioactive decay. It is related to the decay constant $\lambda$ by

$$ t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda} \approx \frac{0.693}{\lambda}. $$

The number of undecayed nuclei $N(t)$ at time $t$, given an initial count $N_0$, obeys the exponential decay law:

$$ N(t) = N_0 \, e^{-\lambda t} = N_0 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{t/t_{1/2}}. $$

The activity $A(t) = \lambda N(t)$ carries the same exponential form and is measured in becquerels ($1\,\text{Bq} = 1\,\text{decay\,s}^{-1}$) or curies ($1\,\text{Ci} = 3.70 \times 10^{10}\,\text{Bq}$).

Worked Example. Iodine-131, used in thyroid treatment, has a half-life of $t_{1/2} = 8.02\,\text{days}$. Suppose a patient receives a dose with an initial activity of $A_0 = 4.00\,\mu\text{Ci}$.

  1. Find $\lambda$: $\lambda = \dfrac{0.693}{8.02\,\text{d}} = 0.0864\,\text{d}^{-1}$.
  2. Activity after $t = 24.06\,\text{days}$ (exactly three half-lives):
$$ A = A_0 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{3} = \frac{4.00\,\mu\text{Ci}}{8} = 0.500\,\mu\text{Ci}. $$

Each successive half-life halves the remaining activity, so after three intervals the dose has decreased to 12.5% of its original value.

Key Theorem. For any radioactive sample, $t_{1/2}$ is independent of $N_0$, temperature, pressure, and chemical bonding state. This universality follows directly from the probabilistic nature of nuclear decay: each nucleus decays with constant probability $\lambda\,dt$ per infinitesimal interval $dt$, yielding $dN = -\lambda N\,dt$ and hence the exponential solution above.

🍃 Decay Constant

🍃 Decay Constant

The decay constant, denoted $\lambda$, quantifies the probability per unit time that a single unstable nucleus will undergo radioactive decay. It is a fundamental, nucleus-specific parameter that governs the rate at which a radioactive sample diminishes over time. Formally, if $N(t)$ represents the number of undecayed nuclei at time $t$, the decay law states:

$$ \frac{dN}{dt} = -\lambda N $$

This first-order differential equation reflects the stochastic nature of radioactive decay: each nucleus decays independently, with no memory of how long it has existed. Solving the equation yields the exponential decay function:

$$ N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t} $$

where $N_0$ is the initial number of nuclei at $t = 0$. The activity $A(t) = \lambda N(t)$ gives the number of decays per second, measured in becquerels (Bq), and it too decays exponentially.

Worked Example. Carbon-14 has a decay constant of $\lambda = 3.83 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{s}^{-1}$. A freshly prepared biological sample contains $N_0 = 6.00 \times 10^{10}$ atoms of $^{14}\text{C}$. Find the activity at $t = 0$ and the number of undecayed nuclei remaining after $t = 5730\ \text{yr} \approx 1.807 \times 10^{11}\ \text{s}$.

Initial activity: $A_0 = \lambda N_0 = (3.83 \times 10^{-12})(6.00 \times 10^{10}) \approx 0.230\ \text{Bq}$.

After $5730$ years: $N = N_0 e^{-\lambda t} = 6.00 \times 10^{10}\, e^{-(3.83\times10^{-12})(1.807\times10^{11})} = 6.00 \times 10^{10}\, e^{-0.692} \approx 3.00 \times 10^{10}$ nuclei. This confirms that $5730$ years is precisely the half-life of $^{14}\text{C}$, since half the original nuclei remain.

Key Theorem. The half-life $t_{1/2}$ is related to the decay constant by:

$$ t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda} \approx \frac{0.693}{\lambda} $$

This relation is derived by setting $N(t_{1/2}) = N_0/2$ in the decay law and solving for $t_{1/2}$.

🍃 Exponential Decay Law

🍃 Exponential Decay Law

Definition. Radioactive decay is a stochastic process in which each unstable nucleus in a sample has a constant, species-specific probability of disintegrating per unit time, called the decay constant $\lambda$ (SI unit: s$^{-1}$). If $N(t)$ denotes the number of undecayed nuclei at time $t$, the macroscopic rate equation is

$$ \frac{dN}{dt} = -\lambda N. $$

Separating variables and integrating from $t = 0$ yields the Exponential Decay Law:

$$ \boxed{N(t) = N_0\, e^{-\lambda t},} $$

where $N_0 = N(0)$ is the initial number of nuclei. The activity $A(t) = \lambda N(t) = A_0 e^{-\lambda t}$ (measured in becquerels, $1\,\text{Bq} = 1\,\text{decay s}^{-1}$) follows the same exponential form. The half-life $t_{1/2}$ is defined by $N(t_{1/2}) = N_0/2$, giving

$$ t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda} \approx \frac{0.6931}{\lambda}. $$

Worked Example. Iodine-131, used in thyroid therapy, has $t_{1/2} = 8.02\,\text{days}$. A patient receives a dose with initial activity $A_0 = 3.70 \times 10^8\,\text{Bq}$. Find the activity after $24.06\,\text{days}$ (exactly three half-lives).

$$ \lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{8.02\,\text{d}} = 0.08642\,\text{d}^{-1}. $$ $$ A(24.06\,\text{d}) = A_0\, e^{-\lambda(24.06)} = 3.70\times10^8 \cdot e^{-3\ln 2} = \frac{3.70\times10^8}{8} = 4.63\times10^7\,\text{Bq}. $$

Each half-life reduces activity by half; three successive halvings yield $1/8$ of the original, confirming the result.

Key Theorem. The mean lifetime $\tau$ of a nucleus is the expectation value of the decay time:

$$ \tau = \int_0^\infty t\,\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\,dt = \frac{1}{\lambda} = \frac{t_{1/2}}{\ln 2}. $$

Thus $N(t) = N_0\,e^{-t/\tau}$, and after one mean lifetime the population falls to $N_0/e \approx 36.8\%$ of its initial value.

Payoff 🎯

Radioactive dating stands as the culminating concept of nuclear physics because it transforms abstract decay mathematics into a precise chronometer capable of measuring time on scales inaccessible to any other physical instrument. By mastering the exponential decay law, half-life calculations, and isotopic abundance ratios, the student arrives at a technique that has resolved foundational questions in geology, archaeology, cosmology, and medicine — fields that would otherwise be forced to rely on relative sequencing rather than absolute chronology.

The technique rests on a deceptively simple observation: a radioactive parent nuclide decays into a stable daughter product at a rate governed solely by quantum mechanical tunneling probability, rendering the decay constant $\lambda$ immune to temperature, pressure, or chemical environment. For a sample of initial activity $A_0$, the number of parent nuclei remaining after time $t$ is

$$ N(t) = N_0 \, e^{-\lambda t}, $$

from which the age follows directly:

$$ t = \frac{1}{\lambda} \ln\!\left(\frac{N_0}{N(t)}\right) = \frac{t_{1/2}}{\ln 2} \ln\!\left(1 + \frac{N_d}{N_p}\right), $$

where $N_d$ and $N_p$ are the measured daughter and parent atom counts and $t_{1/2}$ is the known half-life.

Worked example. A granite sample contains $^{238}$U ($t_{1/2} = 4.47 \times 10^9\,\text{yr}$) and its daughter $^{206}$Pb in a ratio $N_d/N_p = 0.0718$. Substituting into the age equation:

$$ t = \frac{4.47 \times 10^9}{\ln 2}\,\ln(1.0718) \approx \frac{4.47 \times 10^9}{0.6931} \times 0.0693 \approx 4.47 \times 10^8\,\text{yr}. $$

The granite solidified roughly 447 million years ago — a result unobtainable without the decay law developed throughout this chapter.

Key theorem (Bateman equation). In a decay chain $A \to B \to C$, the daughter population satisfies

$$ N_B(t) = N_{A,0}\,\frac{\lambda_A}{\lambda_B - \lambda_A}\!\left(e^{-\lambda_A t} - e^{-\lambda_B t}\right), $$

which underlies isochron dating methods that bypass the need to know $N_0$ directly, eliminating a major source of systematic error.
