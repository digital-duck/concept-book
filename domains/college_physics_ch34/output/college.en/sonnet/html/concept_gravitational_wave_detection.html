🌸 Gravitational Wave Detection

🌸 Gravitational Wave Detection

Definition. Gravitational waves are ripples in spacetime curvature, predicted by Einstein's general theory of relativity, produced when massive objects undergo rapid acceleration. They propagate at the speed of light $c$ and are characterized by a dimensionless strain amplitude

$$ h = \frac{\Delta L}{L}, $$

where $\Delta L$ is the change in proper length of a detector arm of rest length $L$. For astrophysical sources, $h$ is extraordinarily small—on the order of $10^{-21}$—necessitating interferometric detection of sub-attometer displacements.

Detection principle. The Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory (LIGO) employs a Michelson interferometer with two perpendicular arms, each $L = 4\,\text{km}$ long. A passing gravitational wave with plus-polarization ($h_+$) stretches one arm by $\delta L_x = \tfrac{1}{2}h_+ L$ while simultaneously compressing the orthogonal arm by $\delta L_y = -\tfrac{1}{2}h_+ L$. The resulting differential optical path length change is

$$ \Delta \ell = 2(\delta L_x - \delta L_y) = 2h_+ L, $$

which shifts the interference fringe at the output port. Fabry–Pérot cavities and power-recycling mirrors amplify the effective arm length to approximately $1600\,\text{km}$, bringing the displacement sensitivity below $10^{-18}\,\text{m}/\sqrt{\text{Hz}}$.

Worked example. The GW150914 event (detected 14 September 2015) produced a peak strain of $h \approx 1.0 \times 10^{-21}$. Estimate the corresponding arm-length change in LIGO:

$$ \Delta L = h \cdot L = (1.0 \times 10^{-21})(4.0 \times 10^{3}\,\text{m}) = 4.0 \times 10^{-18}\,\text{m}. $$

This displacement is roughly $4 \times 10^{-3}$ times the classical radius of a proton ($r_p \approx 10^{-15}\,\text{m}$), illustrating the extraordinary sensitivity required.

Key theorem (quadrupole formula). To leading order, the gravitational wave power radiated by a system with time-varying mass quadrupole moment $Q_{ij}$ is

$$ P = \frac{G}{5c^5}\left\langle \dddot{Q}_{ij}\,\dddot{Q}^{ij}\right\rangle, $$

known as the Peters–Mathews formula. It predicts inspiral rates that have been confirmed both by binary pulsar timing (Hulse–Taylor pulsar, 1974) and by direct LIGO detections.

Lab exercise. Using the quadrupole formula, estimate the gravitational wave frequency at merger for two equal-mass neutron stars, each of mass $M = 1.4\,M_\odot$, when their orbital separation equals $4R$ where $R = 10\,\text{km}$ is the stellar radius. Apply Kepler's third law to find the orbital angular frequency $\omega_{\operatorname{rm} orb}$, then note that $f_{\operatorname{rm} GW} = 2f_{\operatorname{rm} orb} = \omega_{\operatorname{rm} orb}/\pi$. Compare your result to LIGO's sensitive band ($10$–$1000\,\text{Hz}$) and comment on detectability.
