🌱 Quantum Tunneling

🌱 Quantum Tunneling

Definition. Quantum tunneling is the phenomenon whereby a quantum-mechanical particle penetrates and traverses a potential energy barrier even when its total mechanical energy is less than the barrier height — a process strictly forbidden by classical mechanics. For a particle of mass $m$ and total energy $E$ encountering a rectangular potential barrier of height $U_0 > E$ and width $L$, the time-independent Schrödinger equation inside the barrier yields a wavefunction of the form

$$ \psi(x) = A e^{\kappa x} + B e^{-\kappa x}, \quad \kappa = \sqrt{\frac{2m(U_0 - E)}{\hbar^2}}, $$

where $\kappa$ is the real decay constant. The exponential attenuation of $|\psi|^2$ through the barrier gives a nonzero transmission probability, or tunneling probability, rather than identically zero amplitude on the far side.

Worked Example. Consider an electron ($m = 9.109 \times 10^{-31}\ \text{kg}$) with kinetic energy $E = 1.0\ \text{eV}$ approaching a barrier of height $U_0 = 5.0\ \text{eV}$ and width $L = 0.50\ \text{nm}$. First compute $\kappa$:

$$ \kappa = \frac{\sqrt{2(9.109\times10^{-31})(4.0 \times 1.602\times10^{-19})}}{1.055\times10^{-34}} \approx 1.025 \times 10^{10}\ \text{m}^{-1}. $$

For a thick barrier the transmission coefficient is well approximated by

$$ T \approx e^{-2\kappa L} = e^{-2(1.025\times10^{10})(5.0\times10^{-10})} = e^{-10.25} \approx 3.5 \times 10^{-5}. $$

Roughly 1 in 30,000 electrons tunnels through — negligible macroscopically, yet measurable and technologically significant.

Key Theorem (WKB Approximation). For a smooth, slowly varying barrier $U(x)$, the Wentzel–Kramers–Brillouin approximation gives the transmission coefficient as

$$ T \approx \exp\!\left(-2\int_{x_1}^{x_2} \kappa(x)\, dx\right), \quad \kappa(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2m[U(x)-E]}{\hbar^2}}, $$

where $x_1$ and $x_2$ are the classical turning points. This result underpins the theory of alpha decay and field-emission devices.

Lab Exercise. Using a scanning tunneling microscope (STM) simulation, vary the tip-to-sample gap $d$ in 0.1 nm increments from 0.3 nm to 0.8 nm and record the tunneling current $I$. Plot $\ln I$ versus $d$ and extract $\kappa$ from the slope. Compare your experimental value with the theoretical prediction for a work function of $\phi = 4.5\ \text{eV}$.
