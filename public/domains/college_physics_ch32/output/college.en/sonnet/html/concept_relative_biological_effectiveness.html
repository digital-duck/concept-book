🍃 Relative Biological Effectiveness

Definition. Not all ionizing radiation produces equal biological damage per unit of absorbed energy. The relative biological effectiveness (RBE) of a given radiation type is defined as the ratio of the absorbed dose of a reference radiation (typically 250 keV X-rays) required to produce a specified biological effect, to the absorbed dose of the radiation under study required to produce the same effect:

$$ \text{RBE} = \frac{D_{\text{ref}}}{D_{\text{test}}}\bigg|_{\text{same effect}} $$

RBE is dimensionless and depends on the radiation type, its energy, the tissue irradiated, and the biological endpoint under consideration. To account for varying biological potency, the biologically effective dose, called the dose equivalent $H$, is defined as

$$ H = \text{RBE} \times D $$

where $D$ is the absorbed dose in grays (Gy, $1\,\text{Gy} = 1\,\text{J/kg}$). The SI unit of dose equivalent is the sievert (Sv); thus $1\,\text{Sv} = 1\,\text{J/kg}$ of RBE-weighted energy deposition.

Worked Example. A radiation worker receives an absorbed dose of $2.0\,\text{mGy}$ from fast neutrons, for which $\text{RBE} = 10$, and simultaneously $5.0\,\text{mGy}$ from gamma rays ($\text{RBE} = 1$). Compute the total dose equivalent.

$$ H_{\text{neutrons}} = 10 \times 2.0\,\text{mGy} = 20\,\text{mSv} $$ $$ H_{\gamma} = 1 \times 5.0\,\text{mGy} = 5.0\,\text{mSv} $$ $$ H_{\text{total}} = 20\,\text{mSv} + 5.0\,\text{mSv} = 25\,\text{mSv} $$

Despite the neutron component carrying less than half the absorbed dose, it contributes 80% of the biological risk.

Key Theorem. The linear energy transfer (LET) of a particle — the energy deposited per unit path length — governs RBE. High-LET radiations (alpha particles, heavy ions, fast neutrons) produce dense ionization tracks that overwhelm cellular DNA repair mechanisms, yielding RBE values ranging from 10 to 20. Low-LET radiations (X-rays, gamma rays, electrons) produce sparse ionization and are assigned $\text{RBE} \approx 1$.

Lab Exercise. Using a Geiger–Müller counter and calibrated radioactive sources, measure the count rates for a $^{60}$Co gamma source and an Am-Be neutron source at equal source activities. With tissue-equivalent phantom data provided by your instructor, calculate the absorbed dose rate in mGy/h for each source. Apply appropriate RBE values ($\text{RBE}_\gamma = 1$, $\text{RBE}_n = 10$) and determine which source presents the greater dose-equivalent rate to a nearby worker. Discuss how shielding material choice differs for each radiation type based on their LET characteristics.
