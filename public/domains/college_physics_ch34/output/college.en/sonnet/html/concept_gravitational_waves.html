🍃 Gravitational Waves

🍃 Gravitational Waves

Definition. Gravitational waves are propagating disturbances in the curvature of spacetime, produced by accelerating masses with a non-zero quadrupole moment. Predicted by Einstein's general theory of relativity in 1916, they travel at the speed of light $c$ and carry energy away from their source. The linearized Einstein field equations in the weak-field, flat-background limit yield a wave equation for the metric perturbation $h_{\mu\nu}$:

$$ \Box \bar{h}_{\mu\nu} = -\frac{16\pi G}{c^4} T_{\mu\nu} $$

where $\bar{h}_{\mu\nu} = h_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac{1}{2}\eta_{\mu\nu}h$ is the trace-reversed perturbation and $\operatorname{Box} = -\partial_t^2/c^2 + \nabla^2$ is the d'Alembertian. In vacuum ($T_{\mu\nu} = 0$), plane-wave solutions take the form $h_{\mu\nu} = A_{\mu\nu} e^{i k_\alpha x^\alpha}$, propagating transversely in two independent polarization states conventionally labeled $h_+$ and $h_\times$.

Worked Example. Consider a binary star system consisting of two equal masses $M = 1.4\,M_\odot$ in a circular orbit of radius $r = 10^9\,\text{m}$. The orbital angular velocity is $\omega = \sqrt{GM/r^3}$. For equal masses, the dominant gravitational-wave frequency is $f_{\operatorname{rm} GW} = 2f_{\operatorname{rm} orb} = \omega/\pi$. Substituting $G = 6.674\times10^{-11}\,\text{N\,m}^2/\text{kg}^2$ and $M_\odot = 1.989\times10^{30}\,\text{kg}$ gives $f_{\operatorname{rm} GW} \approx 0.13\,\text{Hz}$, placing the signal in the millihertz band targeted by space-based detectors such as LISA.

Key Theorem — Quadrupole Formula. The total power radiated in gravitational waves is given by the quadrupole formula:

$$ P = -\frac{G}{5c^5} \left\langle \dddot{Q}_{ij} \dddot{Q}^{ij} \right\rangle $$

where $Q_{ij} = \int \rho\!\left(x_i x_j - \tfrac{1}{3}\delta_{ij}r^2\right)dV$ is the traceless mass quadrupole tensor and overdots denote time derivatives. This result confirms that spherically symmetric oscillations produce no radiation, and that energy loss causes binary orbits to inspiral — a prediction confirmed by the 1974 Hulse–Taylor pulsar observations.

Lab Exercise. LIGO strain data for the GW150914 event is publicly available through the Gravitational Wave Open Science Center. Download the $H_1$ channel time series, apply a 35–350 Hz bandpass filter, and compute the power spectral density using Welch's method. Identify the frequency at peak strain. Then, using the chirp mass formula $\mathcal{M} = (m_1 m_2)^{3/5}/(m_1+m_2)^{1/5}$ and the measured peak frequency $f_{\operatorname{rm} peak}$, estimate the chirp mass of the merging black holes and compare your result to the published value $\mathcal{M} \approx 28.3\,M_\odot$.
