🌱 Special Relativity

🌱 Special Relativity

Definition. Special relativity, formulated by Albert Einstein in 1905, rests on two postulates: (1) the laws of physics are identical in all inertial reference frames, and (2) the speed of light in a vacuum, $c \approx 2.998 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s}$, is the same for all observers regardless of the motion of the source or observer. These postulates force a revision of Newtonian mechanics, replacing absolute time and space with a unified spacetime in which simultaneity is relative. The Lorentz factor $\gamma$ governs the magnitude of relativistic effects:

$$ \gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}, \qquad v < c. $$

Two cardinal consequences follow: time dilation, $\Delta t = \gamma\, \Delta t_0$, where $\Delta t_0$ is the proper time measured in the rest frame of the event; and length contraction, $L = L_0/\gamma$, where $L_0$ is the proper length along the direction of motion.

Worked Example. A muon produced in the upper atmosphere travels toward Earth at $v = 0.98c$. Its rest-frame lifetime is $\tau_0 = 2.2\ \mu\text{s}$. In the Earth frame, the observed lifetime is

$$ \tau = \gamma\,\tau_0 = \frac{2.2\ \mu\text{s}}{\sqrt{1 - (0.98)^2}} = \frac{2.2\ \mu\text{s}}{0.199} \approx 11.1\ \mu\text{s}. $$

Without time dilation, a muon traveling at $0.98c$ could cover only $0.98c \times 2.2\ \mu\text{s} \approx 645\ \text{m}$, far less than the $\sim 15\ \text{km}$ atmospheric depth. With dilation, the covered distance becomes $0.98c \times 11.1\ \mu\text{s} \approx 3.26\ \text{km}$—consistent with observed muon fluxes at sea level.

Key Theorem — Relativistic Energy-Momentum Relation. The total relativistic energy and momentum of a particle of rest mass $m$ satisfy

$$ E^2 = (pc)^2 + (mc^2)^2. $$

For a particle at rest ($p = 0$), this reduces to Einstein's celebrated $E = mc^2$. The quantity $mc^2$ is called the rest energy; kinetic energy is $K = (\gamma - 1)mc^2$.

🍃 Equivalence Principle

🍃 Equivalence Principle

Definition

The equivalence principle, central to Einstein's general theory of relativity, asserts that the effects of gravitational acceleration are locally indistinguishable from the effects of inertial acceleration. More precisely, in any sufficiently small region of spacetime, a uniformly accelerating reference frame is physically equivalent to a frame at rest in a uniform gravitational field. This leads to two related formulations: the weak equivalence principle states that inertial mass $m_i$ and gravitational mass $m_g$ are identical for all bodies, so that $m_i = m_g$; the strong equivalence principle extends this to include all laws of physics, asserting that no local experiment can distinguish between gravitational and inertial effects.

Because $m_i = m_g$, Newton's second law $F = m_i a$ combined with the gravitational force $F = m_g g$ immediately yields $a = g$ for all objects regardless of composition — the origin of Galileo's observation that bodies fall at equal rates in vacuum.

Worked Example

Consider an astronaut inside a sealed, windowless spacecraft accelerating at $a = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ in deep space, far from any gravitational source. If the astronaut releases a ball from rest (relative to the spacecraft), the ball appears to fall toward the floor with acceleration $g_{\text{eff}} = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$. By the equivalence principle, every mechanical, optical, and electromagnetic measurement the astronaut performs yields results identical to those obtained in a stationary laboratory on Earth's surface. No internal experiment can reveal whether the acceleration arises from thrust or gravity.

Key Theorem

A direct consequence is the gravitational redshift. A photon emitted with frequency $f_0$ at the floor of an accelerating elevator and received at the ceiling, a height $h$ above, is observed at frequency

$$ f = f_0\!\left(1 - \frac{gh}{c^2}\right), $$

where $c$ is the speed of light. Since the situation is locally equivalent to a gravitational field of strength $g$, an identical redshift must occur for photons climbing out of a gravitational potential well — confirmed experimentally by Pound and Rebka in 1959 using the Mössbauer effect over a height of $22.5\ \text{m}$.

Lab Exercise

Using a precision scale, measure the apparent weight of a $0.500\ \text{kg}$ mass inside an elevator at rest, during upward acceleration $a = 2.00\ \text{m/s}^2$, and during free fall. Record the normal force $N$ in each case and verify that $N = m(g + a)$, $N = mg$, and $N = 0$, respectively. 🍃 General Relativity

🍃 General Relativity

Definition. General relativity (GR), formulated by Albert Einstein in 1915, is a geometric theory of gravitation that supersedes Newtonian gravity. Its central premise is the equivalence principle: the effects of gravitational acceleration are locally indistinguishable from those of inertial acceleration. GR encodes gravity not as a force but as the curvature of four-dimensional spacetime caused by the presence of mass and energy. The governing equations are the Einstein field equations:

$$ G_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} = \frac{8\pi G}{c^4} T_{\mu\nu} $$

where $G_{\mu\nu} = R_{\mu\nu} - \frac{1}{2}Rg_{\mu\nu}$ is the Einstein tensor (encoding spacetime curvature via the Ricci tensor $R_{\mu\nu}$ and Ricci scalar $R$), $\Lambda$ is the cosmological constant, $g_{\mu\nu}$ is the metric tensor, $T_{\mu\nu}$ is the stress-energy tensor representing matter and energy content, and $G$ is Newton's gravitational constant.

Worked Example: Gravitational Time Dilation. A clock on Earth's surface (gravitational potential $\phi_1 = -GM_\oplus/R_\oplus$) and a GPS satellite at altitude $h$ (potential $\phi_2 = -GM_\oplus/(R_\oplus + h)$) run at different rates. The Schwarzschild metric predicts the fractional time difference:

$$ \frac{\Delta t_{\text{surface}}}{\Delta t_{\text{satellite}}} \approx 1 - \frac{\Delta\phi}{c^2} $$

For $h = 20{,}200\ \text{km}$, $\Delta\phi/c^2 \approx 5.3 \times 10^{-10}$, giving a rate discrepancy of roughly $45\ \mu\text{s/day}$. Without GR corrections, GPS positional errors would accumulate at approximately $11\ \text{km/day}$.

Key Theorem: Geodesic Equation. In curved spacetime, freely falling particles (no non-gravitational forces) follow geodesics — the generalization of straight lines. The geodesic equation is:

$$ \frac{d^2 x^\mu}{d\tau^2} + \Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}\frac{dx^\alpha}{d\tau}\frac{dx^\beta}{d\tau} = 0 $$

where $\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}$ are the Christoffel symbols derived from the metric, and $\tau$ is proper time. In the weak-field, slow-motion limit, this reduces exactly to Newton's second law under gravitational acceleration.

Lab Exercise. Compare each result to the object's actual radius and discuss what it would mean physically if a body were compressed to within $r_s$.

🍃 Gravitational Waves

🍃 Gravitational Waves

Definition. Gravitational waves are propagating disturbances in the curvature of spacetime, produced by accelerating masses with a non-zero quadrupole moment. Predicted by Einstein's general theory of relativity in 1916, they travel at the speed of light $c$ and carry energy away from their source. The linearized Einstein field equations in the weak-field, flat-background limit yield a wave equation for the metric perturbation $h_{\mu\nu}$:

$$ \Box \bar{h}_{\mu\nu} = -\frac{16\pi G}{c^4} T_{\mu\nu} $$

where $\bar{h}_{\mu\nu} = h_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac{1}{2}\eta_{\mu\nu}h$ is the trace-reversed perturbation and $\operatorname{Box} = -\partial_t^2/c^2 + \nabla^2$ is the d'Alembertian. In vacuum ($T_{\mu\nu} = 0$), plane-wave solutions take the form $h_{\mu\nu} = A_{\mu\nu} e^{i k_\alpha x^\alpha}$, propagating transversely in two independent polarization states conventionally labeled $h_+$ and $h_\times$.

Worked Example. Consider a binary star system consisting of two equal masses $M = 1.4\,M_\odot$ in a circular orbit of radius $r = 10^9\,\text{m}$. The orbital angular velocity is $\omega = \sqrt{GM/r^3}$. For equal masses, the dominant gravitational-wave frequency is $f_{\operatorname{rm} GW} = 2f_{\operatorname{rm} orb} = \omega/\pi$. Substituting $G = 6.674\times10^{-11}\,\text{N\,m}^2/\text{kg}^2$ and $M_\odot = 1.989\times10^{30}\,\text{kg}$ gives $f_{\operatorname{rm} GW} \approx 0.13\,\text{Hz}$, placing the signal in the millihertz band targeted by space-based detectors such as LISA.

Key Theorem — Quadrupole Formula. The total power radiated in gravitational waves is given by the quadrupole formula:

$$ P = -\frac{G}{5c^5} \left\langle \dddot{Q}_{ij} \dddot{Q}^{ij} \right\rangle $$

where $Q_{ij} = \int \rho\!\left(x_i x_j - \tfrac{1}{3}\delta_{ij}r^2\right)dV$ is the traceless mass quadrupole tensor and overdots denote time derivatives. This result confirms that spherically symmetric oscillations produce no radiation, and that energy loss causes binary orbits to inspiral — a prediction confirmed by the 1974 Hulse–Taylor pulsar observations.

🌸 Gravitational Wave Detection

🌸 Gravitational Wave Detection

Definition. Gravitational waves are ripples in spacetime curvature, predicted by Einstein's general theory of relativity, produced when massive objects undergo rapid acceleration. They propagate at the speed of light $c$ and are characterized by a dimensionless strain amplitude

$$ h = \frac{\Delta L}{L}, $$

where $\Delta L$ is the change in proper length of a detector arm of rest length $L$. For astrophysical sources, $h$ is extraordinarily small—on the order of $10^{-21}$—necessitating interferometric detection of sub-attometer displacements.

Detection principle. The Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory (LIGO) employs a Michelson interferometer with two perpendicular arms, each $L = 4\,\text{km}$ long. A passing gravitational wave with plus-polarization ($h_+$) stretches one arm by $\delta L_x = \tfrac{1}{2}h_+ L$ while simultaneously compressing the orthogonal arm by $\delta L_y = -\tfrac{1}{2}h_+ L$. The resulting differential optical path length change is

$$ \Delta \ell = 2(\delta L_x - \delta L_y) = 2h_+ L, $$

which shifts the interference fringe at the output port. Fabry–Pérot cavities and power-recycling mirrors amplify the effective arm length to approximately $1600\,\text{km}$, bringing the displacement sensitivity below $10^{-18}\,\text{m}/\sqrt{\text{Hz}}$.

Worked example. The GW150914 event (detected 14 September 2015) produced a peak strain of $h \approx 1.0 \times 10^{-21}$. Estimate the corresponding arm-length change in LIGO:

$$ \Delta L = h \cdot L = (1.0 \times 10^{-21})(4.0 \times 10^{3}\,\text{m}) = 4.0 \times 10^{-18}\,\text{m}. $$

This displacement is roughly $4 \times 10^{-3}$ times the classical radius of a proton ($r_p \approx 10^{-15}\,\text{m}$), illustrating the extraordinary sensitivity required.

Key theorem (quadrupole formula). To leading order, the gravitational wave power radiated by a system with time-varying mass quadrupole moment $Q_{ij}$ is

$$ P = \frac{G}{5c^5}\left\langle \dddot{Q}_{ij}\,\dddot{Q}^{ij}\right\rangle, $$

known as the Peters–Mathews formula. It predicts inspiral rates that have been confirmed both by binary pulsar timing (Hulse–Taylor pulsar, 1974) and by direct LIGO detections.

Payoff 🎯

Gravitational wave detection represents the culmination of a century-long theoretical prediction becoming observational reality. When Einstein formulated general relativity in 1915, he derived that accelerating masses produce ripples in spacetime — perturbations propagating at the speed of light, compressing and stretching the fabric of space itself. The strain $h = \Delta L / L$, where $\Delta L$ is the change in arm length and $L$ is the baseline length, encodes information about distant cataclysmic events. For the first detection by LIGO in 2015 (GW150914), this strain reached $h \approx 10^{-21}$, requiring measurement of a displacement smaller than one-thousandth the diameter of a proton across a 4-kilometer interferometer arm.

The physics underlying detection synthesizes every major theme of modern physics encountered in this course. Maxwell's equations govern the laser light propagating through the Fabry-Pérot cavities. The photoelectric effect and quantum optics set the fundamental shot-noise floor $S_n^{1/2} \propto \sqrt{\hbar \omega / P}$, where $P$ is the laser power. Special relativity constrains signal propagation, while Heisenberg's uncertainty principle introduces quantum back-action noise at high power — a direct laboratory encounter with quantum measurement theory. Thermal noise in mirror coatings, seismic isolation stacks, and Newtonian gravity gradient noise each require mitigation through techniques spanning condensed matter physics, mechanical engineering, and geophysics.

The endpoint is natural because gravitational wave astronomy opens an entirely new observational window on the universe. Electromagnetic astronomy observes photons; neutrino detectors sample weakly-interacting particles; but gravitational wave observatories detect the geometry of spacetime itself. Events invisible to light — the merger of two black holes deep inside dense stellar clusters — announce themselves unmistakably through their metric perturbations.

Worked estimate: For two merging black holes each of mass $M = 30\,M_\odot$ at distance $r = 400\,\text{Mpc}$, the characteristic strain is $h \sim \frac{4G\mathcal{M}^{5/3}(\pi f)^{2/3}}{c^4 r}$, where $\mathcal{M}$ is the chirp mass. Substituting $\mathcal{M} \approx 26\,M_\odot$ and $f \approx 150\,\text{Hz}$ yields $h \sim 10^{-21}$, consistent with LIGO's measurement.

Key theorem: The quadrupole formula $\dot{E} = -\frac{G}{5c^5}\langle \dddot{Q}_{ij}\dddot{Q}^{ij}\rangle$ relates the gravitational wave luminosity to the third time-derivative of the mass quadrupole moment tensor, establishing the fundamental source–field relationship.
